# Book I: The Obsidian Eye
## Chapter 1 — The Bookshop of Returns

Barcelona had been rinsed clean by rain that afternoon, though the rain had come too hard and too early for October.

It sluiced down the glass fronts of the new shops in the Raval, turned the pavements black, and drove people under awnings with their shoulders raised around their ears. Scooters hissed through the gutters. Delivery riders bent against the wind. Above them, the apartment balconies held their ironwork and washing lines as they had held them for more than a hundred years, patient and dark against the storm.

Geronimo stood under the narrow shelter of a pharmacy doorway and looked again at the piece of paper in his hand.

The address was written in blue ink.

**Carrer de la Lluna, 17. Ask for the book that was never catalogued. Come alone.**

No name. No phone number. No explanation for why the paper had been tucked beneath the windscreen wiper of his car, under a sheet of wet yellow leaves, when he had parked it outside Katia’s building that morning.

He should have thrown it away.

He had almost done exactly that. Then he had seen the small mark under the address: a black circle crossed by a pale line, as if someone had pressed the tip of a burnt coin into the paper.

The mark had made the back of his neck tighten.

He had not seen it for years. Not clearly. Not outside the blurred edge of childhood memory.

His mother had drawn it once on the steam of a bathroom mirror. He had been small enough to stand on a stool beside her, wrapped in a towel, while she dried his hair with a rough white cloth. She had made the circle with one finger, cut it with a line, and said, *If you ever see this somewhere it should not be, do not follow it alone.*

Then she had smiled as if it were a game.

Two months later she was dead.

Geronimo folded the paper again. His fingers were cold, though the rain was not.

The street numbers ran upward in uneven steps: a tattoo studio, a closed florist, a building wrapped in scaffolding. At number fifteen, the modern city stopped pretending it had replaced the old one. There was no shopfront at seventeen. Only a door of peeling green wood between two walls painted the colour of old bone.

Above the door was a sign so faded that the letters had become shadows.

**LLIBRERIA VALLMANYA. COMPRA I VENDA DE LLIBRES VELLS.**

Buy and sell old books.

The display window held no books. It was filled with dust, a bronze magnifying lens, three cracked porcelain birds, and a clock with no hands. Behind the glass, rainwater blurred the reflection of Geronimo’s face until he looked like someone standing underwater.

He checked the address one more time.

The door opened before he touched it.

There was no bell. No scrape of a lock. One moment it was shut; the next it stood inward on darkness.

Warm, dry air reached him carrying the smell of paper, beeswax and something sharper underneath—stone after lightning.

“You are late,” said a woman’s voice.

Geronimo stepped inside.

The bookshop was deeper than the building had any right to be. Shelves climbed all the way to a ceiling lost in shadow. They leaned at strange angles, connected by narrow ladders and wooden bridges. Books lay flat on the floor in islands, on chairs, on the steps of a small staircase at the rear. Nothing was arranged for display. Nothing looked valuable. Yet the room had the heavy, watchful silence of a place where every object had been kept because it had survived something.

At a counter beneath a green-shaded lamp sat an old woman in a dark cardigan. Her hair was white but not thin; it was braided low at her neck. Her hands rested together on the polished wood. They were small hands, freckled and steady.

She looked at him for a long time.

Not with surprise. With recognition.

“I do not know you,” Geronimo said.

“No,” she replied. “Not in the way you mean.”

Her Catalan was soft, older than the street outside. Not the fast city speech he heard in cafés and markets, but something slower, carefully placed.

He felt irritation rise, welcome in its simplicity. “You sent the note?”

“I made sure it reached you.”

“Why?”

“Because the book has waited long enough.”

He glanced around the shop. “What book?”

The old woman’s expression altered almost imperceptibly. For one second, grief moved through it. Then it was gone.

“The one your mother told me never to sell.”

The words struck harder than they should have. Geronimo gripped the edge of the counter.

“Do not talk about my mother.”

“I would prefer not to.”

“You knew her?”

“I knew a woman who had your eyes when she was afraid.”

The shop seemed to contract. Rain battered the window behind him. Somewhere above, wood clicked in the walls.

Geronimo had spent years learning the shape of his mother’s absence. It was usually manageable. It lived in photographs, in birthdays, in the strange guilty relief of forgetting the exact sound of her laugh. It did not belong in the mouth of a stranger.

“Who are you?” he asked.

The old woman looked down at the counter. “A poor keeper of promises.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No. It is the only honest one I have today.”

“Today?” He heard how close his voice was to breaking and hated it. “You send a message that pulls me out of the rain, tell me my mother left a book here, and that is what you give me?”

The woman did not flinch. “Your mother did not leave a book here. She left a decision. The book is what remains when a decision has been waited on for too long.”

He gave a short, disbelieving laugh. “That sentence means nothing.”

“It will.”

“You knew her. Start there. Was she here? In this shop?”

“Often.” The woman looked past him to the rain-black window. “She came before you were born. She came after you were born. The last time she came, she brought a suitcase she could not carry and a child she would not abandon.”

Geronimo felt the counter under his fingers, hard and solid. “She died when I was little.”

“I know.”

“My father said it was an accident.”

“Your father has said many useful things.”

The anger arrived so quickly it almost steadied him. “Do you know where he is?”

For the first time, real fear showed in her face. “No one knows where he is when he is himself.”

He stared at her.

She shut her eyes briefly, as though she had already spoken too much. “Listen carefully. There are things on Sardinia that have been hidden, traded, prayed over, buried, and fought for. They are not treasures. They do not make anyone special. They make old grief audible. Your mother understood that too late.”

“Sardinia?”

“The island is not the danger. It is the place where the danger remembers its name.”

The phrase should have sounded theatrical. Instead, it sank into him with the weight of an inherited warning. His mother had spoken of Sardinia only once, so casually he had almost forgotten it: a blue line in an old atlas, her fingertip resting near a shape like a broken shield. *One day,* she had said, *you must go somewhere that does not know you yet.*

“What is your name?” he asked.

The old woman’s mouth softened, not into a smile but the shadow of one. “Elena is safe enough for tonight.”

“Safe enough?”

“Names are doors. Some people have been trying to open mine for a very long time.”

The shop’s front bell rang then—one clean, bright note that seemed far too loud. Elena’s head turned sharply. A second later the bell rang again, though nobody had entered.

“Take the key,” she said.

“I am not going upstairs until you tell me why my father—”

“Because if they take the book, he will come closer. If you take it, he will know you are alive.”

The words struck a colder chord than the rain.

“I am obviously alive.”

“Not to him.”

She caught his wrist. Her hand was dry and unexpectedly strong. On the inside of her wrist, beneath the cardigan cuff, he saw a thin scar shaped almost exactly like the black circle on the iron key.

“Your mother made him believe you were beyond his reach,” Elena said. “She gave up almost everything to do it. Do not make her sacrifice into a curiosity.”

The bell rang a third time.

Elena released him and pushed the key across the counter.

She rose. She was shorter than he had thought, and she moved with a slight drag of her left foot. On the counter where her hands had been lay a key tied with red thread. The key was old iron, longer than his palm. A small black circle had been painted on its bow.

“Follow the staircase,” she said. “Do not open anything with your hands until you see your own name.”

Geronimo stared at the key.

“And if I leave?”

“Then someone else will find it.”

“Who?”

This time she did not answer. She turned away and went through a curtain behind the counter.

Geronimo remained where he was.

The sensible thing would be to walk out. Call Katia. Call the police, perhaps, though what would he say? An old woman in a bookshop said she knew my dead mother. She gave me an iron key.

His phone vibrated.

**Katia:** *Are you still coming? Maris has brought enough food for an army and is offended that nobody is eating it.*

He looked at the message. Behind it, as the rain flickered across the window, a second notification appeared.

**Unknown caller:** *Do not take the book upstairs.*

It vanished before he could open it.

The green lamp above the counter went out.

At the same instant, someone knocked on the shop door.

Not the ordinary knock of a customer. Three slow blows. A pause. Then three more.

Geronimo lifted his head.

From behind the curtain, the old woman said sharply, “Upstairs. Now.”

The door handle began to turn.

He took the key.

The staircase at the back of the shop was narrower than it had looked. It wound upward between shelves in a tight spiral, its steps worn hollow in the middle. Geronimo climbed fast, trying not to look down. The floor beneath him felt less like wood than the deck of a boat moving under bad weather.

Below, the shop door opened.

“Good afternoon,” said a man.

His voice was pleasant. Educated. He spoke Spanish with no trace of Barcelona in it.

Geronimo froze between two landings.

“We are looking for a volume,” the man continued. “It is overdue.”

“Books cannot be overdue,” the old woman replied.

“Everything can be overdue.”

Another voice said, “There was a young man outside.”

The first man’s reply was quiet. “Then he has made our task easier.”

Geronimo climbed again.

At the top, the staircase ended at a corridor so low he had to bend his head. Rain hammered the roof above it. Three doors stood along the left wall. The first was painted blue, the second red, the third white. Each had a different lock.

The key in his hand had black paint on it.

There was no black door.

He swore under his breath and checked the walls. Dust lay thick everywhere except on one narrow stretch between the red door and the white. It looked like a seam in the wood. He pressed it. Nothing happened.

Downstairs, a chair overturned.

“You should not have hidden it from us,” the pleasant man said.

The old woman laughed once. There was no humour in it. “You still believe hiding and keeping are the same thing. That is why you have failed every time.”

Geronimo’s pulse stumbled.

Every time.

He put the iron key against the seam. It did not fit. He tried the circle on the key against the wall, feeling ridiculous. The black paint was warm.

His mother’s voice came back to him without warning, not the memory of it this time but the actual small melody she used to hum while cooking. Four notes, then a pause. Four notes climbing toward a fifth that never resolved.

Geronimo had not heard it in years.

The seam opened beneath the key.

Not wide. Only enough to reveal a vertical slit of darkness and the dry smell of earth.

He stepped through as the corridor lights flashed below him.

The hidden room was no larger than a wardrobe. It contained a single table, a broken-backed chair, and a leather-bound book lying beneath a glass dome. No title marked its cover. Rainwater ticked somewhere inside the walls, but the book was completely dry.

On the glass was written one line in white grease pencil.

**GERONIMO HOPPENBROUWERS—DO NOT OPEN UNTIL THE DOOR HAS CLOSED.**

The sound of feet reached the landing outside.

He shut the hidden panel. It sealed at once, cutting off the light.

For a moment he was blind.

Then the table began to glow.

Not brightly. The book gave off a deep, low darkness, a blacker black than the room around it. At the centre of its cover lay the same circle-and-line mark, pressed into the leather like a closed eye.

Geronimo remembered the message: *Do not take the book upstairs.*

He had not brought it upstairs. He had found it.

This did not make him feel safer.

Outside the panel, someone struck the wood.

“Open the door,” the pleasant man said.

Geronimo lifted the glass dome. It was heavier than it looked. The moment it came free, the book opened by itself.

The pages turned with a sound like wings beating underwater.

He saw handwriting. Not one hand, but many. Dark brown ink. Blue ink. Faded red. Lines crossed out and written again. Place names came and went too fast for him to read—**Cagliari**, **Nora**, **Cabras**, **Oliena**, **Santa Cristina**, **Tavolara**—until the pages stopped on a map of Sardinia.

It was not a proper map. The coast had been drawn in a child’s uncertain line. Twelve circles marked the island. Eleven were blank.

The twelfth lay near Alghero.

At the centre of the circle was a smear of black, as if someone had crushed an insect there.

Below the map, in handwriting he recognized with a violence that made him catch his breath, were six words.

**Do not let him make me return.**

His mother’s writing.

The ink darkened while he watched.

A line ran from the black circle near Alghero, descending the map in a crooked path. It stopped at a second empty circle near the western coast, then continued in fragments: a salt-blue lagoon; a knot of red mountains; a thin stone tower at the edge of the sea; a black well under a hard white sun. None of the images held still long enough to become a route. They arrived like memories being forced through a keyhole.

Then the map folded itself.

Geronimo heard wind. Not the rain above Barcelona, but a warmer wind pressing through dry grass. For one breath he saw a pale road across Sardinia, its surface broken by cart tracks and hoofprints. On one side stood ancient stone towers under a moonless sky. On the other, a woman with a lantern waited at the mouth of a grave cut into rock. Her face was not his mother’s, yet when she lifted her head he felt the same impossible recognition.

“You always come late,” she said.

The scene changed.

He stood on a cliff over water so clear the depth of it made him dizzy. A small boat was being driven against the rocks below. Someone in the boat was screaming a name. Beside Geronimo, a boy with dark wet hair held a small object in both hands—a disk of bone or ivory, marked with the black eye. The boy turned. His eyes were his father’s eyes, but young, desperate, and unfamiliar.

“It is not a map to a place,” the boy said. “It is a map to the moment we ruined.”

The sea rose in a wall.

Geronimo blinked and was back in the hidden room, gripping the edges of the table so hard his fingers hurt.

The book lay still. The island was flat on the page again.

He did not know which was worse: that the vision had felt real, or that part of him had understood it.

There was no hidden treasure waiting at the end of this. There was a past arranged like a trap, and it had recognized him.

The room vanished.

Geronimo stood in a kitchen full of morning light.

He was seven years old, but he was also himself. He could see both versions of his hands: the small ones sticky with jam, the adult ones clenched at his sides. His mother stood at the sink in a yellow dress he had forgotten she owned. She was humming the unfinished melody. A suitcase lay open on the table behind her.

“Mum?” he said.

She stopped humming.

For one impossible second she looked directly at him.

Not at the child near the table. At him.

Her face changed. Fear came first. Then love so fierce it hurt to see.

“Geronimo,” she whispered.

The kitchen door opened.

A man stepped in carrying a black stone in both hands.

Geronimo could not see his face. The light bent away from it. But he knew the man’s posture—the narrow shoulders, the familiar way he held his head before speaking.

His father.

“It is almost finished,” the man said.

His mother backed away from the sink. “No.”

“You promised.”

“I promised before I understood what you were asking.”

The black stone moved in his father’s hands. A thin pale line opened across it.

Geronimo’s mother looked past the man, toward the adult Geronimo standing outside time.

“When he comes,” she said, and this time her voice filled the room, “tell him I chose him.”

The kitchen exploded into rain.

Geronimo slammed back against the table in the hidden room. Pain flashed through his shoulder. The open book lay before him. Blood ran from his nose onto the map.

Outside, the hidden panel splintered.

He grabbed the book.

The room had no other exit. The back wall, however, was no longer wood. It had become the outline of a doorway filled with the same blackness that had glowed from the cover.

He knew it was wrong. He knew every instinct was telling him that a dark door appearing in a sealed room was not a door any sensible person should use.

The panel broke inward.

Geronimo stepped through.

He did not fall through another world. He fell down a flight of hard stairs into a narrow service passage that smelled of damp brick and old electricity. The blackness vanished behind him. The book struck the floor, skidded, and stopped against a metal gate.

Above, the bookshop groaned.

He heard shouting. The old woman’s voice. A sharp crash. Then the wild alarm of a smoke detector.

He picked up the book and ran.

The passage led under the buildings, not through them. Pipes crossed overhead. Water dripped onto his hair and jacket. Somewhere close, a rat moved in the dark. The service gate at the far end was chained but not locked; he forced it open and emerged into a rubbish-strewn alley behind a bakery.

For a moment the normal city blinded him. A delivery van reversed with a beeping alarm. Steam curled from a vent. A man in a yellow raincoat shouted into his phone. Geronimo bent over, one hand on his knee, trying to breathe.

His phone was ringing.

Katia.

He answered at once.

“Geronimo?” she said. “Where are you? You sound awful.”

“I need you to send me Maris’s address.”

“You know Maris’s address.”

“Just send it.”

Silence.

Katia did not waste silence. “Are you in danger?”

He looked back toward the alley entrance. Two men came around the corner from the main street. Neither was in a hurry. One wore a long charcoal coat despite the rain. The other had an umbrella he did not open.

“Yes,” Geronimo said.

“Get in a taxi. Do not go home. Do not bring anyone to my place. Maris’s. Ten minutes.”

“Katia—”

“Move.”

He moved.

He ran out into the traffic, nearly collided with a cyclist, and raised his arm at the first taxi he saw. The driver swore at him through the window, then saw his face and unlocked the door.

“Carrer de l’Allada-Vermell,” Geronimo said, sliding into the back seat. “Fast, please.”

The taxi pulled away. Through the wet rear window he saw the two men reach the alley mouth. The man in the charcoal coat watched the cab disappear. He did not chase it.

He smiled.

Geronimo sat forward. “Can you take another route?”

The driver glanced at him in the mirror. “Another route from what?”

“From them.”

The driver looked back. “I do not know who them are.”

“Good.”

At the next corner the driver turned away from the broad avenue and into a string of narrow streets where balconies leaned close enough to pass a secret from one window to another.

“Not the address,” Geronimo said.

“There is a demonstration ahead. And an ambulance.”

“How do you know where I’m going?”

The driver’s hands tightened on the wheel. “You said El Born.”

Geronimo had not. He had only said, *Katia sent an address.*

Before he could answer, the navigation screen on the dashboard went black. A small black circle appeared in its centre, with a white line through it.

The driver swore and slapped the screen. “What is that?”

The same mark glimmered on the book’s cover.

Geronimo snatched it into his lap. The screen came back at once, blank except for the route. The driver looked at him in the mirror and, this time, said nothing at all.

Outside, a church bell began to strike the hour. Each note was swallowed by the rain before the next one came.

Geronimo pressed his thumb against the book’s cover until the leather bit his skin. He wanted to throw it from the moving cab, to call Katia and ask her to say the whole thing aloud was ridiculous, to return to the shop and force Elena to explain every word. Instead he watched the black mark fade and understood, with a sick certainty, that it had not appeared for the driver. It had appeared because something inside the book had recognized the road.

The city passed in broken reflections. The book lay on the seat beside him, strapped in by the taxi’s unused safety belt. He tried not to look at it. He failed every few seconds.

At the next red light, it opened.

Geronimo reached for it, then stopped.

The page showed no map now. It showed a photograph.

It had been taken in the bookshop.

The old woman stood behind the counter, younger by decades, with dark hair and the same steady hands. Beside her stood Geronimo’s mother. Between them, on the counter, lay a sleeping infant wrapped in a blue blanket.

Geronimo knew the blanket. It was folded in a box in his wardrobe at home.

Behind the two women, half-hidden by a ladder, stood a man with his face scratched out in black ink.

Under the photograph, in his mother’s writing, was a date.

The date was three years before Geronimo was born.

The traffic light changed.

The taxi lurched forward.

And on the page, beneath the date, new words began to appear.

**THE EYE HAS OPENED. HE KNOWS YOU ARE ALIVE.**
