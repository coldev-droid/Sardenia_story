# Book I: Chapter 1 — The Cold Horizon

The water in Port Vell was the color of standing oil, catching the restless amber glare of Barcelona’s waterfront and the high, white floodlights of the container terminals beneath Montjuïc. 

On the aft deck of the *Sentina*—a fifty-four-foot, steel-hulled ketch with double headsails and a heavy midship cockpit—Geronimo stood at the helm, his calloused palms resting on the teak-rimmed wheel. Below the hull, the dark water slapped against the anti-fouling paint in a slow, rhythmic cadence, echoing the low thrum of the auxiliary diesel engine idling in neutral. 

"Lines clear forward, Geronimo," André called out from the bow. His tall frame was framed against the bright, loom-lit sky of the city. He slung a thick coiled warp over a deck cleat and stepped back, his heavy boots sounding flat against the teak decking. "Spring line is off the bollard."

"Understood," Geronimo replied, his voice calm and low. He shifted the throttle forward just enough to engage the bronze propeller. The *Sentina* vibrated gently, her heavy keel cutting a smooth bow-wave through the black harbor surface as she glided away from the quay.

Beside the hatchway, Maris sat on a low deck locker, holding the leashes of Mia and Tina. The two dogs—large, short-haired Belgian Malinois with deep chests and dark, alert eyes—were unusually quiet. Normally eager for the movement of the boat, they sat pressed against Maris’s knees, their ears swiveling toward the companionway hatch that led down into the main cabin. Tina let out a faint, whining breath, her nose quivering as she caught a drift of air from below decks.

"Steady, girl," Maris murmured, stroking Tina’s flank. Her fingers sank into the dense fur. "We’re out soon."

Katia emerged from the nav station below, her face lit from beneath by the pale blue glow of a handheld GPS and the warm yellow light of the companionway lamp. She stepped into the cockpit, carrying a laminated paper chart of the Balearic Sea.

"Clearance from Barcelona Port Control on Channel 12," Katia said, checking her watch. "Traffic out of the Llobregat delta is light. Once we clear the outer breakwater and the southern entrance—Bocana Sud—we take a heading of one hundred and twelve degrees east-southeast. That will carry us clear of Mallorca’s northern shelf and set us straight across the deep basin toward the south of Sardinia."

"Wind?" Geronimo asked, glancing up at the wind vane at the masthead.

"Garbí from the southwest, around twelve knots, but it’s predicted to back to the north before midnight," Katia replied. She leaned over the chart table built into the cockpit bulkhead, pinning the paper down with a brass parallel ruler. "If the sea stays flat, we should make seven knots under main and Genoa once we lay our course."

Inga and Veerle came up the steps behind Katia, carrying two thermal flasks and a canvas bag of sea-rations. Inga, her pale hair pulled back into a tight knot at the nape of her neck, squinted back toward the receding shore. The illuminated slope of Montjuïc Castle sat dark against the sky, its yellow beacon sweeping in slow, silent circles across the outer roadstead. Past the long stone barrier of the breakwater, the iconic sail-shaped outline of the W Barcelona hotel glowed against the dark edge of Barceloneta beach.

"Everything locked down in the galley?" Inga asked, looking at Katia and then toward Geronimo. 

"Pots are lashed, sea-cocks closed, deadlights dogging down," Veerle said. She leaned against the mizzen shroud, her gaze dropping to the heavy, iron-strapped chest bolted beneath the main salon table below decks. Though it was completely out of sight from the cockpit, everyone on board was intensely aware of what lay inside it.

The object had been brought aboard three hours after midnight under a heavy tarpaulin: a slab of jet-black volcanic glass, jagged and dense, roughly two feet long and weighing nearly forty pounds. It was known simply as the Obsidian Splinter. It did not belong to any recorded geological formation in Catalonia or the Spanish mainland. Its origin was locked somewhere in the deep basaltic heart of the central Mediterranean, and its presence on the *Sentina* was the sole reason seven people and two dogs were heading out into the dark expanse of the open sea.

"Let’s get the main up," Geronimo said, bringing the boat’s head slowly into the light breeze as they cleared the red port-hand marker at the end of the breakwater. 

André and Inga moved to the halyards at the base of the mainmast. The heavy Dacron sail rose smoothly up the track, snapping once as it caught the air before André sweated the line home on the bronze winch and jammed the stopper shut. Geronimo brought the helm round, falling off toward the open water. The diesel engine was cut, and a sudden, profound quiet descended over the vessel, broken only by the hiss of water along the run of the hull and the creaking of the boom against the traveler.

Barcelona receded into a glittering strip of land-bound lights. The city’s noise—the distant roar of coastal traffic, the hum of the port—was swallowed by the dark, cold vastness of the Balearic Sea.

***

By two hours after midnight, the coastline of Spain had dissolved entirely into the western horizon. The sky above was clear, crowded with cold, sharp stars, but the sea below was pitch black, undulating in long, slow swells that lifted the *Sentina*’s stern and set her down with a heavy, deliberate roll.

Geronimo was still at the helm. Beside him in the cockpit, Katia was monitoring the radar display, while Veerle sat across from them, taking notes in a leather-bound logbook by the red glow of a headlamp. 

Below decks, the atmosphere had begun to alter.

It started not with a sound or a movement, but with a sudden, unnatural drop in temperature.

Maris, who was sitting in the midship berth trying to catch an hour of sleep, woke up with her breath clouding in the dark. She sat up rapidly, her boots hitting the cabin floorboards. The air inside the cabin had turned icy—not the damp, chilly salt air of a Mediterranean night, but a dry, biting, artificial cold that seemed to radiate directly from the floorboards.

Beside her berth, Mia and Tina were standing stiff-legged, their hackles raised along their spines. They were not barking, but making a low, shuddering clicking noise in their throats, their ears pinned flat against their skulls.

"Geronimo?" Maris called out, her voice raspy. She pushed back her blanket and reached for her jacket. The moment she stood up, a sudden, blinding light-headedness hit her. 

She stopped, leaning her hand against the mahogany bulkhead to steady herself. A strange, hollow sensation bloomed behind her forehead, as if a hand had reached into her skull and smoothed out the folds of her brain. She looked down at her hands, then at the wooden companionway ladder three paces away.

*What was I doing?* she thought.

The question did not come with panic; it came with a eerie, quiet blankness. She looked at the two dogs standing at her feet. She knew they were animals, and she knew she was responsible for them, but for a terrible, stretched three seconds, their names vanished from her mind entirely. She stared at the black Malinois by her knee, her lips working silently.

*Mia. Tina.* 

The names returned slowly, like cold grease thinned by a weak flame, but the effort to recall them left a dull, aching throb behind her eyes.

"Inga!" Maris called out, stepping toward the main salon. "Inga, check the bilge heater! The temp is dropping!"

In the galley, Inga was standing by the stove, holding an unlit box of matches. She was staring blankly at the cold steel ring of the burner. 

When Maris entered, Inga turned to her. Her cheeks were pale, and her breath rose in thick white plumes against the dim galley lamp. 

"Inga?" Maris asked, frowning. "What’s wrong?"

Inga blinked slowly. Her eyes were wide, dilated, reflecting the single bulb above the sink. "I... I was going to light the stove," she said softly. Her voice sounded distant, flat, stripped of its usual crisp precision.

"Why?"

"I don't know," Inga whispered. She looked at the box of matches in her fingers as if she had never seen wood or sulfur before. "I knew why a minute ago. I had a reason. We were... we were making something."

"Hot tea," Maris said, reaching out to touch Inga’s arm. The fabric of Inga’s woolen sweater was ice-cold to the touch, as if she had been standing outside in a blizzard rather than inside the insulated salon of a steel yacht. "Inga, your sleeve is freezing."

On the table in the middle of the main cabin sat the iron-bound trunk. 

The heavy padlocks securing the iron straps were coated in a delicate, geometric frost. White needles of ice had begun to grow outward along the dark grain of the oak planks, radiating from the center of the chest where the Obsidian Splinter rested inside its bed of lead and wool. The temperature near the table was noticeably colder than by the galley sink—a freezing micro-climate that seemed to draw the ambient warmth out of the wood, the air, and the very bodies of the people in the room.

Up in the cockpit, the air was also changing.

Geronimo pulled his collar up around his neck, feeling a sudden, razor-thin draft cut across the back of his neck. The wind had not changed direction; the Garbí was still blowing steadily from the southwest at eleven knots. Yet the air rising up out of the companionway hatch was bitter, carrying the distinct, sharp smell of supercooled stone and frozen iron.

"Katia," Geronimo said, his voice tightening. "Check the chart. What's our current position?"

Katia leaned over the plotter. She reached out her pencil to mark the lat-long coordinates on the paper chart, but her hand hovered two inches above the paper. Her fingers twitched.

"Katia?"

She didn't answer. She was staring at the paper. The printed lines of latitude, the soundings marked in fathoms and meters, the compass roses showing magnetic variation—all of it seemed to be shifting under her eyes, losing their meaning.

"Katia!" Geronimo said louder, reaching out with one hand to shake her shoulder.

She started, looking up at him with blank, unfocused eyes. "I... I know where we are," she stammered. Her lips were blue. "We are... we left..."

She stopped. A look of sheer terror crossed her face. 

"We left where, Katia?" Geronimo asked, his own pulse quickening as a wave of numbing cold seemed to creep up from the deck floorboards into the soles of his boots.

"I don't know," she whispered. Her voice broke. "I know the numbers. Three, nine, degree, four, zero... but I don't know the name of the port. I don't know the name of the land behind us."

"Barcelona," André said from the starboard side deck, his voice coming short and fast as he stepped back into the cockpit. He was shivering violently, his arms wrapped tight around his chest. "It’s Barcelona, Katia. We left Barcelona four hours ago."

"Did we?" Veerle asked from the corner of the cockpit. She had dropped her pen. It had rolled into the scupper. She was staring at her hands, turning them over and over in the moonlight. "Then why can’t I see it? Why can’t I remember the quay? I remember the water... I remember the sound of the diesel... but the town is gone. The streets are gone."

Geronimo gripped the wheel tighter. The cold was moving through him now—not just through his muscle and skin, but into the deep architecture of his memory. He tried to think of his father’s house, of the wooden dock where he had learned to tie a bowline as a child, of the broad square where they had loaded the supplies two days ago. 

The thoughts were there, but as the ambient temperature fell, they grew brittle, cracking like frozen film. The harder he tried to hold onto a specific detail—a face, a street name, the date—the faster it receded into a grey, frost-rimmed fog.

*Thermal amnesia,* he realized with a jolt of cold horror. 

It was not an infection of the mind; it was a physical extraction. The artifact in the hold was not merely absorbing heat from the air; it was consuming the thermal energy of the vessel, and with that heat went the energetic friction of human thought. The colder the room became, the slower the mind ran, until memory froze entirely, leaving only the immediate, sensory present.

"André!" Geronimo barked, his teeth chattering so hard his jaw ached. "Down below! Get the wool blankets from the forepeak! Cover the trunk!"

"It’s... it’s locked in lead!" André said, his face pale under the starlight.

"It doesn't matter! Put insulation over it! Fire up the emergency diesel heater if the lines aren't frozen! Move!"

André stumbled down the companionway steps, nearly slipping on the frost-sheened teak treads. 

Below decks, the scene was uncanny. The cabin lamp was dim, its flame flickering weakly in the oxygen-dense, freezing air. Inga was sitting on the cabin floor with her back against the starboard locker, her hands pulled deep into her sleeves, staring fixedly at the frosted trunk. Maris was on her knees beside the dogs, wrapping her own heavy coat around Mia and Tina, who were huddled together, shuddering rhythmically.

"André..." Maris gasped, her breath hanging like smoke in the small cabin. "I can't... I can't remember my sister's face."

"Don't try," André gritted out through clenched teeth. He plunged forward into the dark forepeak cabin, ripping thick wool emergency blankets from the storage lockers. "Don't think about it! Keep your mind on what’s in your hands! Hold the dogs! Touch the wool!"

He threw three heavy, grey wool blankets over the frosted iron-bound trunk. 

The moment the wool settled over the box, a low, muffled hiss echoed through the cabin, like water hitting a hot stove in reverse. The frost on the outer iron bands did not melt; instead, it thickened instantly, binding the wool fibers into a stiff, frozen board. But the rapid loss of radiant cold from the surrounding air slowed.

André knelt beside the stove, taking a copper pocket lighter from his vest. His thumb was numb, stiff, barely able to strike the flint. Once, twice—a shower of orange sparks fell uselessly onto the cold steel. On the third strike, a small yellow flame erupted from the wick.

He held the flame close to his chest, feeling the tiny, fragile circle of heat radiating against his chin and cheek.

"Inga," he called out, his voice shaking. "Come here. Look at the fire."

Inga crawled across the floorboards, her movements slow, mechanical. She reached her shivering hands out toward the tiny yellow flame of the lighter.

As the heat touched her skin, her eyes cleared slightly. She sucked in a deep, agonizing breath, as if emerging from deep water.

"Barcelona," she whispered, her lips trembling as warmth returned to her face. "The port... the lights on the hill. We were carrying the Splinter away from the coast."

"Yes," André said, holding the flame steady until the fuel began to sputter. "We are carrying it east."

Up in the cockpit, the razor-sharp chill began to dull, returning to the normal, cool dampness of the Mediterranean night. 

Geronimo felt the fog in his brain thin. The grey void that had threatened to swallow his thoughts receded, leaving behind a cold, metallic ache at the base of his skull. He looked down at Katia. She was slumped against the cockpit coaming, her breathing fast and shallow, but her eyes were focused once again on the paper chart pinned to the table.

"Katia," Geronimo said quietly. "Are you back?"

She looked up at him, wiping a thin sheen of frost-melt from her forehead. She looked down at the chart, touched the plotted line with a trembling index finger, and nodded once.

"One hundred and twelve degrees," she said, her voice small, but clear. "We are forty miles off the coast. Deep basin."

"Keep her on course," Geronimo said, turning his gaze back out over the black, featureless ocean ahead. 

Behind them, the coast of Spain was completely invisible, buried beneath four hours of deep water and the quiet, terrible cold they carried in their wake. Ahead lay hundreds of miles of open sea, and somewhere beyond the dark horizon, the ancient, silent granite of Sardinia waited for them.
