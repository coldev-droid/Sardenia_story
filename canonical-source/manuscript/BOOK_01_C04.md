# BOOK I: THE OBSIDIAN EYE
## CHAPTER 4: THE ROAD OF THE JANAS

Sardinia did not rise from the sea like a revelation. It emerged as a gray, bruised line of rock through a curtain of freezing autumn mist. 

There was no triumphant speech on the upper decks of the *Cruise Roma*. There was only the bone-deep exhaustion of a night spent bracing against the erratic heave of a storm-battered ferry, the pervasive reek of diesel exhaust, and the sharp, metallic smell of wet mooring ropes. The heavy north-west wind cut across the Gulf of Asinara, driving sheets of rain against the thick, salt-crusted glass of the observation lounge. 

Geronimo sat with his hands wrapped around a paper cup of bitter espresso that had long since gone cold. Beside him on the heavy vinyl bench, the waterproof case containing the book and the Obsidian Eye rested against his thigh. He felt the cold radiation of it, a localized pressure like a drop in barometric gravity, pressing against his hip. 

On the floor, Mia and Tina lay curled together in a tight, miserable knot of damp fur. They were shivering. The vibration of the ferry’s massive engines switching to reverse thrusters sent tremors through the steel deck, causing the dogs’ claws to scrabble uselessly for purchase. Katia knelt beside them, speaking in a low, steady voice, her left hand awkwardly bracing her aggravated wrist against her knee. 

"We are docking," Maris said. She stood by the window, her phone held up to the glass as if trying to physically extract a signal from the gray sky. "Porto Torres. The crew just confirmed. The storm delay cost us three hours, but we are making a normal landing."

"Good," Inga said. She was packing the last of their scattered belongings into the canvas duffels. Her movements were tight, pragmatic, stripping away the vulnerability of the night. "Normal is exactly what we need right now."

Geronimo looked down at the waterproof case. Inside, he knew the black glass of the Eye was attempting to dictate their path. During the worst of the storm, he had opened the case a fraction of an inch, just enough to see the spine of the ancient codex. The word *Nora*—which the Echo had traced onto the page back in Barcelona—was glowing with a faint, oily luminescence. Below it, a jagged black line had begun to project itself onto the page, attempting to map a direct, ruthless path due south through the island’s interior. 

"The book wants us to go south immediately," Geronimo murmured, his voice cracking from disuse. "It is drawing a line."

Maris lowered her phone and turned to him. The fluorescent lights of the lounge cast sharp shadows under her eyes. "Let it draw whatever it wants, Geronimo. I just checked the official regional alerts. The Oristano province and the central arteries took a massive hit from the storm. The direct roads south are flooded or blocked by mudslides. We are not following a magical line into a washout."

"The pact holds," Veerle added softly, stepping up beside Maris. "We move with the landscape, not against it. The Eye can mark a route, but it cannot command our transport."

Geronimo nodded, feeling a flush of relief. The sheer pragmatism of these women was the only thing tethering him to the earth. 

As the ferry pivoted in the churning water of the harbour, the coastline of Porto Torres swung into view. It was a working port, defined by towering industrial cranes, vast concrete breakwaters, and the sprawling, layered geometry of human trade. 

André stood at the adjacent window, his forehead resting against the cold glass. His eyes were fixed on the town rising behind the docks. Through the driving rain, the silhouette of a long, low Romanesque basilica stood in stark relief against the gray sky, flanked by a sturdy stone tower. 

"San Gavino," André whispered.

Katia looked up from the dogs. "You know it?"

André frowned, rubbing his temples as if trying to soothe an ache. "I... I don't know. The shape of it." He blinked, his gaze distant. "My father brought me here once, I think. Or he showed me a photograph. I remember a white building near the water, and that tower." His voice dropped to a hollow murmur. "*A port is where a promise becomes cargo.*"

"What does that mean?" Inga asked, pausing with a duffel bag in her hands. 

"I don't know," André said, shaking his head sharply. "It's just a fragment. Something he said. It doesn't prove anything."

"We document it, but we don't build a strategy on it," Maris decided. "First priority: we get off this boat, we get the dogs onto solid ground, and we find transport that isn't dependent on maritime schedules."

The disembarkation was a chaotic, freezing ordeal. The vehicle deck was a cavern of idling engines and exhaust fumes. They walked down the steel ramp as foot passengers, leaning hard into the wind, the dogs struggling to keep their footing on the slick metal grating. 

Inside the terminal building, the air was thick with the smell of wet wool and the sound of multilingual announcements echoing off the concrete walls. Geronimo kept a tight grip on the waterproof case, his eyes scanning the sparse crowd. 

"Watch that man by the exit doors," Veerle murmured, leaning close to Geronimo. 

Geronimo looked. A man in a high-visibility vest, holding a clipboard, was standing unusually still near the sliding glass doors, his eyes tracking the disembarking passengers with mechanical precision. He wasn't looking at luggage; he was looking at faces. 

"A Sentinel?" Geronimo asked, his stomach tightening. 

"A spotter, at least," Veerle said. "The Father has human contacts. Port authorities, dock workers. People who owe debts or favor the influence of money over questions. We verify, but we assume we are being watched."

"Keep your heads down," Maris instructed. "Let’s find a driver."

The terminal’s taxi rank was largely empty, the local drivers having retreated from the morning deluge. Only one transport remained: a battered, heavy-duty passenger van with a local livery. The driver, a broad-shouldered man smoking a damp cigarette under the awning, looked them up and down as they approached.

"We need to get to Alghero," Maris said in clear, commanding Italian. 

The driver flicked his cigarette onto the wet tarmac. "It will be expensive. Cash only. The main roads are a mess. I can take the interior route, the provincial road south through the valleys. It’s faster. We bypass Sassari completely."

Geronimo felt the case against his leg hum. The black line he had seen projected in the book aligned perfectly with the driver’s suggestion—a straight, ruthless cut south through the interior. *Faster,* the Eye seemed to whisper. *Direct.*

"No," Katia said sharply. She stepped forward, her healthy hand resting protectively on Mia’s wet head. "The dogs are exhausted, they need a smooth ride, and my wrist cannot take being thrown around on a battered provincial backroad. We take the SS131 to Sassari, and then the main highway across to Alghero."

The driver scowled. "That takes an hour longer. The Sassari ring road is crawling with traffic right now."

"We will pay for the extra hour," Maris said, pulling a thick roll of Euros from her jacket. "The safe road. Or we wait here and find someone else."

The driver looked at the cash, then at the shivering dogs. He sighed, pocketing the money. "Fine. Sassari it is. Load the animals in the back."

The drive was an exercise in endurance. The van’s wipers thumped a frantic, rhythmic beat against the deluge. The interior smelled intensely of wet dog and stale tobacco. Outside the windows, the Sardinian landscape was a blur of dark, soaked earth, leaning cork oaks, and jagged limestone outcroppings. 

Geronimo watched the rain streak the glass. He could feel the Obsidian Eye sulking in its case, a heavy, inert weight of disapproval. It wanted the direct path. It wanted the flooded road. 

By the time they reached Alghero, the storm had broken, leaving behind a sky of bruised purple and a fierce, cleansing wind. The city sat on a fortified peninsula, its massive sea walls built of golden stone holding back the churning Mediterranean. The streets were slick with rain, reflecting the yellow light of the streetlamps that were just beginning to flicker on in the gloom. 

They secured rooms in a small, licensed guesthouse hidden in a narrow cobblestone alley. The courtyard smelled of wet rosemary and sea salt. Once the dogs were dried, fed, and sleeping heavily on the terracotta floor, Maris and Inga set up a tactical perimeter, checking the locks and the street sightlines. 

"We need a local orientation," Katia said, wrapping her wrist in a fresh support bandage. "We have the word *Nora*, and we have whatever that black line was trying to show us. But we don't know how to read the landscape."

Veerle nodded. "I know someone. An antiquarian bookseller and local historian in the old town. He doesn't ask questions about where a book comes from, only what it says."

An hour later, Geronimo, Katia, and Veerle stood in the cramped, humid interior of a bookshop carved into the ancient Catalan walls of Alghero. The air smelled of binding glue, sea damp, and old paper. The owner, a thin man named Paolo with spectacles perched on the edge of his nose, was examining the waterproof case. 

"You don't want to open it," Veerle advised quietly. "Just look at the photograph we took of the page."

Paolo adjusted his glasses and stared at the high-resolution image on Katia's phone. It showed the word *Nora* and the jagged black line terminating at a strange, geometric symbol that looked like a squared-off archway. 

"This is old cartography," Paolo murmured, switching between Italian and a fluid, rolling Catalan-Sardinian dialect. "The place-name pattern... it's not a direct map. It's a conceptual one. *Nora* is a Phoenician and Roman city in the far south. It's the end of the line. But this symbol here, where the line pauses..." He tapped the screen. "That isn't a map pin. That is an architectural cross-section."

"Of what?" Geronimo asked. 

"A doorway," Paolo said. "Specifically, the entrance shaft of a *domus de janas*."

Geronimo frowned. "A house of fairies?"

Paolo smiled, a sad, dry expression. "That is what the grandmothers tell the children. That the little square holes carved into the limestone cliffs are where the fairies live. But you are scholars, yes? You know what they really are."

"They are Neolithic tombs," Katia said softly. "Rock-cut necropolises. Houses for the dead."

"Exactly," Paolo said. "The people who carved them, five thousand years ago, shaped the interiors to look like the homes of the living. Roof beams, pillars, false doors—all carved from solid rock. They gave the dead a house so they would not wander. To call it a *domus de janas* is to preserve the mystery, but also to enforce respect. You do not disrespect the house of a fairy, and you do not loot the house of the dead."

"The book uses the phrase as an ethical clue," Veerle realized. "It isn't pointing to a vault of treasure. It's pointing to a burial site. A place requiring lawful, respectful access."

"There is a massive necropolis very close to here," Paolo offered. "Anghelu Ruju. The largest concentration of these tombs in the north. If this symbol is a doorway, that is where it leads."

Geronimo felt a cold thrill of recognition. *Anghelu Ruju*. 

"What about the legend of Sardus Pater?" Geronimo asked, recalling a fragment from his own historical research. "The ancient founder, the mythic father of the Sardinian people. Is there a connection?"

Paolo shrugged. "A Roman invention overlaid on an indigenous deity. People use the myth of Sardus Pater to claim ancient authority, a 'chosen bloodline' that entitles them to rule the island. It is a political fiction masquerading as ancient magic."

"A lie used to justify power," Veerle said coldly. "We reject that entirely. Bloodlines do not give you the right to open tombs."

They returned to the guesthouse as true night fell. The wind was howling off the sea, rattling the wooden shutters. In the small courtyard, sheltered from the worst of the gale, they gathered around a heavy wooden table. 

Geronimo placed the waterproof case on the table. He looked at the faces of his cell—Katia, exhausted but resolute; Maris and Inga, watchful and armed with a cold pragmatism; André, silent and searching his own fragmented memories; Veerle, the ethical anchor. And the dogs, breathing steadily by the radiator. 

"The book requires a commitment before it will show us the true path to Anghelu Ruju," Geronimo said, his voice barely audible over the wind. "Not blood. Not power. A boundary."

He opened the case. The book lay inside, its damaged spine exposed. The black glass of the Obsidian Eye stared up at them, inert and cold. 

A single drop of rainwater, blown in from the courtyard eaves, fell from the edge of the roof and landed directly on the ancient leather cover of the codex. 

The water did not soak in. It beaded on the surface, trembling. 

"State what you will not sacrifice," Geronimo said. 

Silence stretched. Then, Katia spoke. "I will not sacrifice the safety of the innocent to uncover the past."

The bead of rainwater elongated, drawing a thin, silver line across the leather. 

"I will not sacrifice the pack for the mission," Maris said. 

The line grew, curving sharply. 

"I will not sacrifice the dignity of the dead to enrich the living," Veerle said. 

The line extended further. 

"I will not sacrifice logic for the comfort of a comfortable lie," André said. 

The line completed its circuit, forming the distinct, six-pointed scratch of a mineral mark—the sign of rock-crystal and bronze. It pulsed once, a clear, geographic confirmation pointing precisely inland, toward the controlled, archaeological site of Anghelu Ruju. 

Magic had been born on the table, not from a spell of power, but from a contract of restraint. 

Geronimo stared at the silver line. But as he looked, his gaze slipped past the leather and fell into the deep, reflective blackness of the Obsidian Eye. 

Instantly, the courtyard vanished. The wind ceased. 

Geronimo found himself standing on a narrow, flooded asphalt road in the dead of night. The water was rushing over his ankles, thick with mud and debris. The air was unnaturally still, heavy with the scent of stagnant water and ozone. 

Ahead of him, walking away into the darkness, was a figure. A woman in a gray coat. 

Geronimo’s breath hitched. He heard a low, melodic humming—a lullaby he hadn't heard in forty years. 

"Mother?" he whispered. 

The figure stopped and began to turn. The grief in Geronimo’s chest expanded, sudden and violent, a physical pain that demanded to be soothed. He took a step forward, the muddy water sloshing around his boots. He just needed to see her face. He just needed to reach her. 

He lifted his foot to take another step. 

A heavy, solid mass slammed against his shins. 

Geronimo stumbled, blinking. The flooded road shattered like fragile glass. 

He was back in the courtyard. The wind was howling. The rain was driving down. 

Mia was standing directly in front of him, her front paws planted firmly on his boots, her golden eyes staring up at him with unblinking intensity. She let out a low, warning *woof*, a sound of pure, grounding reality. 

Geronimo gasped, staggering backward, clutching the edge of the table. He was trembling violently. The Obsidian Eye sat in its case, a dead, black stone once more. 

"Geronimo!" Katia was at his side instantly, her healthy hand gripping his arm. "What happened? You went completely rigid. You were walking toward the gate."

Geronimo swallowed hard, his heart hammering against his ribs. "The Eye... it showed me a road. The flooded road the driver wanted to take. It showed me my mother walking on it. It wanted me to follow her."

Maris walked over, her face grim in the yellow courtyard light. She held up her phone, the screen illuminated with a bright red regional alert. 

"I just checked the transit updates," Maris said, her voice tight. "That provincial road south? The one the driver called 'faster'? Thirty minutes ago, a flash flood washed out the central bridge. Two cars went into the ravine. The road is a death trap."

Geronimo stared at the phone, then down at the Obsidian Eye. 

It had magnified his grief, weaponized his deepest longing, and used it to project the most seductive apparition in the world—all to lead him directly into a ditch of rushing, lethal water. Magic could not overrule terrain, and it could not overrule the brutal physics of a flash flood. 

He reached down and rested his trembling hand on Mia’s head, feeling the coarse, wet fur and the steady, living warmth of the animal beneath. 

"Close the case," Geronimo whispered, choosing, for the first time since Barcelona, to entirely trust the living hands pulling him back. "We go to Anghelu Ruju in the morning."
