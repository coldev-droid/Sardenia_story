# BOOK I — CHAPTER 2: THE EMERALD ABYSS

The engine’s rhythmic thrum vibrated through the steel hull of the expedition vessel, a steady, low-frequency hum that seemed to sync with the slow roll of the swell. They had cleared the Balearic shelf hours before midnight, leaving the distant, fading loom of Majorca behind them, pushing east-northeast across the open stretch of the Balearic Sea toward the western coast of Sardinia. 

At the helm, Geronimo kept his eyes fixed on the dark horizon where sea met sky in an unbroken seam of ink. Beside him on the bridge, the radar screen swept its pale green vector across forty nautical miles of empty water. The digital bathymetry readout fluctuated steadily: three thousand meters, three thousand two hundred, three thousand one hundred and fifty. They were traversing the Algero-Provençal abyssal plain, a vast oceanic trench where the seabed lay buried beneath millions of years of sediment and deep-water gloom.

In the companionway below, the claw-tap of paws announced the arrival of Mia and Tina. The two dogs—Mia, a lean Belgian Malinois with a black muzzle, and Tina, a rough-coated terrier mix—ascended the steps with an unusual, stiff-legged hesitation. Mia did not come to rest her chin against Geronimo’s knee as she usually did on night watches; instead, her ears were pinned back, her nostrils flaring as she sampled the conditioned air circulating through the bridge vents. Tina crouched low against the teak deckhead, her tail tucked tightly between her hind legs, her dark eyes darting toward the instrument panel.

"They feel the pressure shift," Maris said softly, entering the bridge behind them with two insulated mugs of black coffee. She handed one to Geronimo and leaned over the chart table where Katia and Veerle were verifying their dead-reckoning plot against the satellite navigation system. "Barometer hasn't dropped, but look at the sea surface temperature probe. It dropped two degrees in less than twenty minutes."

"It's not just temperature," Katia said, without looking up from the spread of hydrographic charts. Her divider points rested on the chart boundary marking the transition into Sardinian territorial waters. "Look at the water clarity. Look at the hull lights."

Geronimo leaned over the starboard rail of the bridge wing and flicked on the high-intensity marine searchlights mounted to the spreader arms. 

The beam cut down into the water, but what it revealed was not the deep, indigo-black typical of the Mediterranean abyssal plain. The sea had changed. Beneath the hull, the surface water had transformed into a luminous, hyper-clear emerald green—so vivid and piercing that it seemed lit from below by an invisible, subterranean incandescent source. The color was not organic; it carried none of the milky turbidity of an algal bloom or the silt-laden murk of a river plume. It was crystalline, cold, and unnaturally deep, allowing the eye to penetrate tens of meters down into the translucent stratum.

"It looks like meltwater," Veerle murmured, joining them on the bridge wing. Her windbreaker rustled in the cool night air. "Or hyper-saline brine."

"It's the bathymetry reacting," Inga said, stepping up from the lower laboratory deck, a handheld refractometer in her hand. She held up the device toward the bridge instrument lights. "I just drew a sample from the sea chest intake. Salinity is off the chart—nearly forty-two parts per thousand. Normal surface salinity here shouldn't exceed thirty-eight."

"The Messinian layer," André whispered from the darkness near the bow. He walked back toward the illuminated bridge, his face pale against his dark fleece. "We're passing over the evaporite salt domes."

They all knew the geology. Nearly six million years ago, during the Messinian Salinity Crisis, the Strait of Gibraltar had closed, severing the Mediterranean from the Atlantic Ocean. Over tens of thousands of years, the sea had evaporated under a relentless prehistoric sun, dropping the water level by kilometers, transforming this vast basin into a scorched, desolate salt desert thousands of feet below global ocean level. Giant rivers carved canyon gorges through the exposed continental margins, leaving behind massive beds of halite, gypsum, and anhydrite hundreds of meters thick. When the Atlantic finally broke back through at Gibraltar in the Zanclean flood, filling the basin in a torrential cataclysm, those ancient salt flats were submerged, buried beneath kilometers of modern marine sediment.

Now, beneath their keel, those ancient salt diapirs—the subterranean pillars of Miocene salt—seemed to be bleeding into the deep water column, altering the water's optical density and refraction, turning the deep sea into an emerald mirror of a prehistoric catastrophe.

"It feels like sailing over a grave," Maris said quietly, staring down into the glowing green void. "A sea that died once already."

Mia let out a low, suppressed whine from inside the wheelhouse. Tina backed away from the doors, her lips pulling back slightly over her teeth in a silent bared-tooth grimace.

"What is it with the dogs?" Geronimo asked, turning back inside and latching the heavy weatherproof door behind him.

"It’s not the water," Katia said, pointing to the radio stack on the overhead console. "It’s the VHF."

The main radio unit—a dual-watch marine VHF transceiver—was illuminated in amber light, tuned to international distress Channel 16 and working Channel 68. The squelch control was dialed high, yet the speaker was not silent. A low, wet static hissed through the speaker, oscillating in rhythmic waves that matched the rolling pattern of the ocean swell.

Suddenly, through the white noise, a voice broke through.

*“…Vessel in position thirty-nine degrees forty minutes North, eight degrees East… do you read…”*

Geronimo stepped to the console and unhooked the microphone. "Vessel calling, this is the research craft. We read you weak and distorted. State your callsign and intention."

There was a long silence, filled only by the rhythmic *shhh-shhh-shhh* of the static. Then the speaker crackled again, clearer this time, but the cadence of the voice made the hairs on the back of Geronimo’s neck stand up.

*“…State your callsign and intention…”* the radio repeated.

It was not a standard repetition. The timbre, the slight rasp at the end of the vowels, the exact inflection—it was Geronimo’s own voice, spoken back to him through the speaker with terrifying fidelity.

"Who is this?" Geronimo demanded, his voice hardening. "Identify your vessel immediately."

*“…Who is this?”* the speaker returned instantly, using Geronimo’s exact words, but now another voice blended underneath it—a light, melodic tone that sounded precisely like Inga. *“…Identify your vessel immediately… we are running dark… open the deck hatch…”*

Inga gasped, her hand flying to her throat. She stepped back until her spine hit the bulkhead. "That's—that's my voice. I never said those words. I haven't spoken into that radio tonight!"

"Cut the power to the VHF," Maris said sharply, stepping toward the console.

"No, wait," Veerle said, her eyes wide as she monitored the radio frequency analyzer display on the secondary screen. "Look at the signal strength meter. It’s not picking up a broadcast transmission from an antenna. There’s no RF signal coming through the aerial on the mast. The hardware itself is vibrating. The speaker cone is oscillating without an incoming radio frequency!"

The dogs began to bark—a sharp, alarmed sound that echoed in the enclosed bridge space. Mia hit the teak deck with her forepaws, staring directly at the overhead radio housing.

The radio speaker crackled again, but the static was no longer white noise. It had taken on a wet, clicking texture, like the sound of dry leaves or fingernails scraping against parchment.

*“…Geronimo…”* the radio whispered. The voice was now André’s, clear and intimate, coming from the speaker while André himself stood paralyzed three feet away. *“…The water is green because the skin is thin here… open the transom door… let us step aboard… we have forgotten how to breathe air…”*

"Turn it off!" André yelled, his voice cracking. "Turn the damn breaker off!"

Katia reached past Geronimo and slammed down the main circuit breaker marked *NAV-COMM 1*. 

The digital display on the radio went dark. The amber light vanished. 

The voice did not stop.

*“…we are already on the working channel…”* the dark speaker cone whispered, its mechanical diaphragm moving without electric current, driven by some impossible, localized physical resonance. *“…the daughters of the shadow… the Cogas walk the sea when the wind dies… give us your names… give us your breath…”*

"It's the *Cogas*," Maris whispered, her face devoid of color. She looked out the bridge window at the vast, emerald-glowing expanse of the Balearic Sea. "In the old lore... in the southern villages... they were the shape-shifters, the nocturnal thieves of breath and form. They take what belongs to the living. They mimic the voices of the household to be invited inside."

"We're forty miles off the coast," Katia said, her voice shaking despite her attempt at scientific detachment. "Witches don't fly across forty miles of open water."

"They don't fly," Geronimo said, looking down into the hyper-saline, emerald clarity beneath the hull. "They swim in the deep strata. They ride the salt."

The air inside the wheelhouse suddenly grew thick, heavy with the sharp, ozone-and-brine smell of stagnant sea caves. The windows began to fog from the outside—not with damp mist, but with tiny, crystalline patterns of salt frost that crept inward from the aluminum window frames, etching delicate, spider-web structures across the safety glass.

Out on the foredeck, a sharp *thump* echoed through the steel hull, followed by another, and another—like wet, heavy objects slapping against the non-skid deck paint.

Mia let out a fierce, protective growl, her chest vibrating against Geronimo’s legs. Tina backed beneath the chart table, trembling violently.

"Stay inside," Geronimo ordered, grabbing a heavy brass hand-spotlight from its bulkhead bracket. He unlatched the bridge door an inch. The air that rushed in was freezing, saturated with the intense, metallic stench of evaporated sea brine.

He shoved the door open and stepped onto the bridge wing, casting the powerful beam of the spotlight down onto the deck below.

The foredeck was wet, glistening with a thick, gelatinous film of hyper-saline water. Near the anchor locker, three distinct wet impressions lay stamped into the deck coating—shaped like long, emaciated human feet with stretched, webbed toes, but trailing off into thin, sinuous smears of greenish slime that led toward the open sea at the bow.

Along the starboard bulwarks, the brass stanchions were vibrating, humming at a high, singing pitch that matched the frequency of the killed radio speaker.

Geronimo swept the spotlight out over the water. 

In the emerald illumination of the underwater lights, figures were visible beneath the surface. They were not fish, nor marine mammals. They were long, slender, pale shapes, moving with a horrifying, undulating grace through the clear green depths. Their hair streamed behind them like dark mats of sea-wrack, and as they crossed the light beam, their faces turned upward.

They had no distinct features—only hollow, darkened sockets where eyes should have been, and wide, pale mouths that opened and closed in perfect, synchronized mimicry of human speech, though no sound reached the surface through the water except the continuous, low humming that vibrated through the steel plates of the hull.

"Geronimo!" Katia called out from the doorway, her voice tight with panic. "The GPS display—it's corrupting!"

Geronimo retreated into the wheelhouse and slammed the heavy door, locking the dogs inside with them. 

On the center console, the vector map was spinning wildly. The latitude and longitude coordinates were cycling through random strings of numbers, flickering uncontrollably before settling into an impossible readout:

`LAT: 00°00'00" N`  
`LON: 00°00'00" E`

"The magnetic compass is spinning too," Veerle said, pointing to the binnacle mounted above the helm. The card was rotating clockwise in a smooth, frictionless rhythm, completely decoupled from the earth's magnetic field.

"They're stripping our reference frame," Maris said. Her hands were clamped onto the edge of the chart table to steady herself. "If we lose our position, if we accept their voices, we won't hit the coast of Sardinia. We'll sail straight down into the Messinian basin."

"No," Geronimo said flatly. He reached down and seized the mechanical throttle levers, disengaging the automated helm control. "We don't rely on the glass."

He looked back at Katia. "Get the paper charts. Get the parallel rulers and the hand-bearing compass. André, go to the engine room hatch—use the mechanical voice pipe, not the intercom. Tell Inga to lock the sea chests and manual cooling valves. We run on internal tank water until we clear this zone."

"And the radio?" Veerle asked, staring at the darkened box that still emitted a low, wet rustling sound—a thousand tiny voices whispering their names in unison from the silent speaker cone.

Geronimo reached into his pocket, pulled out a heavy steel marlinspike, and jammed it straight through the center of the speaker grille, puncture-fracturing the internal paper cone and shorting the coil against the housing. 

The whispering stopped instantly. A sharp, dying hiss echoed through the metal frame, and then the wheelhouse was left with only the deep, mechanical throb of the diesel engine and the heavy breathing of the eight occupants and two dogs.

"Steer two-hundred-and-seventy degrees relative to the swell," Geronimo commanded, gripping the manual wooden wheel. "We don't trust the lights. We don't trust the water. We steer by the iron of the hull and the weight of the sea."

He shoved the throttles forward. The engine roared in response, the twin screws biting into the dense, hyper-saline water below. The vessel surged forward, her bow cutting through the unnaturally clear emerald sea, throwing up high, sparkling rooster-tails of salt-heavy spray that crusty-dried instantly as it struck the deckhouses.

For two agonizing hours, they maintained their course in complete electromagnetic silence. No one spoke into a microphone; no electronic instrument was consulted. Katia stood by the open window with a traditional pelorus, sighting off the faint, ancient stars that managed to pierce through the high atmospheric haze above.

Gradually, the unnatural emerald hue beneath the keel began to fade, deepening back into the familiar, comforting dark indigo of the abyssal plain. The salinity readings on the manual hydrometer dropped back toward thirty-eight parts per thousand. The salt-frost on the bridge windows melted into streaks of plain ocean grime.

At 0400 hours, the first dark, jagged silhouette broke the eastern horizon under the dim glow of a waning moon.

It was the western headlands of Sardinia—the towering, slate-black cliffs of the Sinis peninsula and the ancient, granite spine of Mount Ferru rising behind them, dark and silent against the night sky.

On the deck below, Mia and Tina finally uncurled themselves from under the chart table. Mia walked slowly to the bridge wing door, pushed her nose against the glass, and let out a single, low bark toward the approaching island.

"We made it across," André said softly, staring at the dark landform emerging from the sea.

"We cleared the water," Geronimo corrected him, his eyes fixed on the looming dark coast. "But the island is where they came from."
