# Book I: The Obsidian Eye
## Chapter 2 — The Hounds of the Ferryman

The taxi stopped at the edge of the pedestrian zone, its tyres hissing against the slick cobblestones of El Born. Geronimo paid the driver without waiting for change, the leather-bound book shoved hard inside his jacket, against his ribs. 

He stepped out into the deluge. The rain in Barcelona had lost its urban rhythm; it was falling now with the heavy, vertical violence of a monsoon, turning the narrow gothic streets into rushing black rivers. He pulled his collar up and began to walk, then jog, checking the mouths of the alleyways.

*THE EYE HAS OPENED. HE KNOWS YOU ARE ALIVE.*

The words burned in his mind, superimposed over the neon reflections in the puddles. He had spent his entire life believing he was ordinary. An architect of minor projects. A man who paid his taxes, loved his friends, and mourned a mother who died too young in a tragic accident. Now, the weight of the book against his chest felt like a detonator.

Carrer de l’Allada-Vermell opened before him, a wide avenue flanked by medieval stonework and tightly packed balconies. Maris’s apartment was on the third floor of a corner building, distinguished by the heavy wrought-iron grating over its heavy wooden door.

He keyed the code, pushed the door open, and took the stairs two at a time. His chest heaved, his lungs burning with a strange, metallic cold.

He didn't knock. He unlocked the door and threw it open.

The apartment was warm, smelling of garlic, roasted peppers, and damp wool. Yellow light spilled across the hardwood floor. 

At the long oak dining table, the group was already gathered. Katia looked up from a spread of architectural blueprints, her dark hair tied back, a smudge of charcoal on her cheek. Maris, broad-shouldered and pragmatic, was uncorking a bottle of red wine. Veerle sat by the window, her sharp, pale eyes already tracking Geronimo's ragged breathing. Inga was leaning against the kitchen counter, arms crossed, while André, usually the loudest in the room, was uncharacteristically quiet, watching the rain beat against the glass.

On the rug by the sofa, Mia and Tina lifted their heads. The two blue American Staffordshire Terriers—usually the first to greet him with wagging tails and low, happy grunts—did not move toward him. 

Mia’s ears pinned flat against her skull. Tina let out a sound Geronimo had never heard from her: a low, vibrating whine that seemed to originate from her bones. They backed away, their claws clicking frantically on the hardwood, wedging themselves between the sofa and the wall.

"Geronimo?" Katia said, dropping her pen. She took in his soaked clothes, the wild look in his eyes, and the blood that had dried beneath his nose. "What happened? Were you mugged?"

"No," he said. His voice sounded thin, completely alien to the warm domesticity of the room. He walked to the center of the table and pulled the book from his jacket. He set it down on the blueprints. It hit the wood with a heavy, unnatural thud that seemed to swallow the ambient noise in the room.

"What is that?" Maris asked, setting the wine down.

"I went to the address on the note," Geronimo said, wiping the rainwater from his eyes. "The bookshop. Carrer de la Lluna. An old woman gave me this."

Veerle stood up slowly. She didn't look at Geronimo; she looked at the book. "That leather is ancient. It looks like it’s been buried."

"It's not just a book," Geronimo said. He felt his hands trembling and forced them flat against the table. "My mother didn't die in an accident. She didn't just leave me a savings account and a few photos. She left me this. And she spent her whole life hiding me."

André pushed off the wall. "Hiding you from who, man? You're not making sense."

"From my father." 

Silence fell over the room. Even the rain outside seemed to pause. Everyone in the room knew the story. Geronimo’s father had abandoned them before he was born. A phantom. A non-entity.

Geronimo reached out and flipped the book open to the page with the photograph. The ink of the message had dried into the parchment, black and jagged. 

Katia leaned in. Her breath hitched. "That's... that's you. The blanket. I've seen it in your flat. And that's your mother."

"And the man with his face scratched out?" Inga asked, her voice dropping.

"Is the man who is hunting us," Geronimo said. "The old woman said the Eye has opened. That he knows I'm alive. Two men followed me from the shop. They weren't ordinary thugs, Katia. They didn't run. They didn't have to."

"Hold on," Maris said, holding up a hand, his logistical brain trying to compartmentalize the madness. "Someone followed you here? To this building?"

As if in answer, the heavy filament bulbs above the dining table flickered. Once. Twice. Then they dimmed to a dull, burning orange.

Tina whined again from behind the sofa, a sound of pure, primal terror. 

Veerle, still standing by the window, went rigid. "Geronimo," she whispered. 

"What?"

"The rain."

Geronimo moved to the glass. Katia followed. 

Outside, the streetlamps of El Born cast a sickly, sodium-yellow glare over the cobblestones. But something was fundamentally wrong with the physics of the storm. The raindrops hitting the windowpane were no longer streaking downward. 

They were sliding *up*.

Water defied gravity, crawling up the glass like thousands of translucent insects. 

"What the hell is that?" André backed away from the window, his bravado evaporating. "Is that a trick? A pressure differential?"

"That’s impossible," Inga said, her voice tight. 

"The book," Veerle said, her eyes wide. "Close it."

Geronimo turned back to the table, but before he could touch the leather cover, the black circle pressed into the spine flared with a cold, absence-of-light aura. The room's temperature plummeted. A layer of frost snapped across the edges of the wine glasses. 

Suddenly, the book’s pages began to turn on their own, a furious rustling that sounded like dry leaves in a gale. It stopped on a new, blank page. 

Blue ink began to bleed upward from the pores of the paper, forming an image with terrifying speed. 

It was a sketch of a shoreline. A dark, jagged coastline under a bloated moon. In the foreground, a woman stood waist-deep in the churning surf. She held a rusted iron comb, dragging it through the water. Attached to the comb were long, shimmering strands of gold—*Byssus*. Sea silk. The legendary fabric of Sardinia, spun from the saliva of the noble pen shell. 

But this wasn't a historical document. As Geronimo stared at the sketch, the water in the drawing *moved*. The waves crested and crashed in silent animation. The woman turned her head, and though her face was obscured by wet hair, Geronimo felt the same agonizing jolt of recognition he had felt in the bookshop's hidden room. 

*His mother.*

Beneath the moving sketch, jagged text slashed across the page:

**THEY HAVE FOUND THE SCENT. CROSS THE WATER. THE FERRYMAN WAITS AT THE GATES OF TARRAGONA. DO NOT LET THEM TOUCH YOUR SHADOW.**

"We have to leave," Geronimo said, slamming the book shut. The frost on the glasses immediately began to melt. The lights surged back to full brightness. 

"Leave?" Maris snapped, his voice rising in panic and anger. "Leave to where? You walk in here with a cursed antique, the laws of gravity break, and you want us to just pack a bag?"

"They know I'm here," Geronimo said, grabbing Katia by the shoulders. "If they followed me, they saw me come into this building. They know you are my friends. The woman in the shop warned me—my father doesn't just kill. He reclaims. He consumes. If you stay here, you are part of the collateral."

A heavy, resonant *thud* echoed up the stairwell outside the apartment. 

It wasn't the sound of footsteps. It sounded like a boulder being dropped on the wooden landing of the first floor. 

*Thud.* 

The second floor landing. 

Mia barked—a sharp, hysterical sound.

"Fire escape," Katia said, her shock giving way to pure adrenaline. She had always been the most grounded of them, the one who saw the geometry of survival. "Maris, grab your go-bag. Inga, get the dog leads."

"Are we seriously doing this?" André yelled, his eyes darting to the locked front door. "We're running from a noise on the stairs?"

*Thud.* 

The noise was on the third-floor landing now. Right outside the door. 

The heavy iron deadbolt on Maris’s door began to turn. No one was touching the key on the inside. The lock was simply grinding open, the metal groaning as unseen torque was applied to it from the hallway.

"Move!" Geronimo roared. 

Maris didn't hesitate anymore. He grabbed a heavy canvas duffel from the hall closet, tossing Katia her raincoat. Inga snapped the leads onto the trembling collars of the two dogs. 

Katia threw open the kitchen window that led to the wrought-iron fire escape. The storm outside was deafening, the rain back to its normal, punishing downward trajectory. 

The front door's deadbolt snapped with a sound like a gunshot. 

The wooden door bulged inward.

Geronimo shoved Veerle and André toward the window. "Go, go, go!" 

Katia climbed out into the rain, slipping on the wet iron. Maris hoisted the dogs out one by one, their muscular bodies slick with rain, before hauling himself onto the grate. Inga and André followed, scrambling down the iron stairs toward the dark alleyway below.

Geronimo was the last one in the kitchen. He slung his waterproof jacket on, zipping the book safely against his chest. 

Behind him, the front door exploded off its hinges. 

It didn't swing open; the entire frame shattered, sending splinters of oak rocketing across the dining room. Geronimo froze, one leg out the window. 

Two men stood in the threshold. It was the same two from the alley behind the bookshop. The one in the charcoal coat, and the one with the unopened umbrella. But now, in the bright light of the apartment, Geronimo saw what his panicked mind had missed earlier. 

The man in the charcoal coat wasn't wet. 

Despite walking through a torrential downpour, his coat was bone dry. His hair was perfectly styled. And when he stepped into the room, he cast no shadow on the floorboard, despite the bright overhead lights.

"Geronimo Hoppenbrouwers," the man said. His voice was smooth, carrying a strange acoustic resonance, as if he were speaking inside a cavern. "You have something that belongs to the Sovereign. The Map of Returns is not a toy for children."

Geronimo didn't answer. He threw himself backward out the window, grabbing the iron railing as he fell onto the fire escape grate. 

He didn't look down. He scrambled down the slick metal stairs, his boots slipping on the narrow rungs. The dogs were already at the bottom, pulling Inga toward the mouth of the alley. 

"Keep moving!" Geronimo shouted over the roar of the rain. "To the main avenue!"

They sprinted through the labyrinth of El Born, a frantic, splashing exodus. They cut through Plaza de Santa Maria, the massive gothic cathedral looming above them like a mountain of black stone. 

"Where are we going?" Maris yelled, keeping pace with Geronimo. 

"The book said the ferryman! The port!" Geronimo shouted back. "We need to get to the terminal!"

"The Grimaldi terminal?" Katia yelled, her hair plastered to her face. "Geronimo, the ferries to Italy and Sardinia leave at night. It's almost ten. If there's a boat, the gates close in twenty minutes!"

"Then we run faster!" 

They burst out onto the Passeig de Colom, the broad boulevard that separated the old city from the Port of Barcelona. The wind coming off the Mediterranean was savage, tearing at their clothes and whipping the palm trees into frantic arcs. 

Across the multi-lane road, the massive industrial infrastructure of the port lit up the night sky. Ferries the size of apartment blocks sat in their berths, their funnels glowing under floodlights. 

"There!" André pointed. A massive, blue-and-white Grimaldi Lines ferry was docked at the far terminal. Its rear cargo ramp was down, but the warning lights were flashing orange—the signal for imminent departure. 

They dodged through the stalled, rain-lashed traffic, horns blaring around them as they sprinted across the asphalt. 

The terminal was chaos. Late passengers were arguing with ticket agents behind the glass booths. Cargo trucks idled in long, diesel-choked lines. 

Maris, operating on pure logistical instinct, shoved to the front of the pedestrian ticket queue. He slammed his passport and a drenched credit card onto the counter. "Six adults. Two dogs. One way. Porto Torres. Now."

The agent blinked, looking at the soaked, desperate group. "Sir, boarding closes in three minutes. I can't process—"

"Do it," Veerle said, stepping up beside Maris. Her voice carried a strange, icy authority that made the agent pause. "Charge whatever tariff you must. But print the boarding passes."

Geronimo looked back toward the avenue. 

Through the sheet of rain, standing on the pedestrian traffic island amidst the gridlocked cars, was the man in the charcoal coat. He wasn't running. He was simply watching them. And next to him, standing perfectly still in the downpour, were three more figures in identical coats. 

The Eye against Geronimo's chest felt like a block of dry ice. 

"Got them!" Maris yelled, snatching the thermal-printed tickets from the machine as it whirred. "Run!"

They sprinted past the security turnstiles, flashing the passes at a bewildered guard, and pounded up the long, enclosed glass gangway that led to the ship. 

The gangway shook under their boots. Below them, the massive hydraulic pistons of the cargo ramp were already beginning to whine, lifting the steel bridge away from the dock. 

They hit the ship's entrance lobby just as a crew member in a yellow high-vis jacket moved to lock the heavy watertight doors. 

"Hold it!" André shouted, throwing his shoulder against the door. 

They spilled into the carpeted lobby of the ferry, gasping, coughing, slipping on the linoleum. Mia and Tina shook themselves violently, spraying water everywhere. 

The crew member stared at them, then spoke into his radio. "Last passengers aboard. Secure the gangway."

With a heavy, mechanical thud, the door sealed shut. The sound of the storm was instantly cut off, replaced by the deep, resonant hum of the ship's massive engines. 

Geronimo slumped against the bulkhead, sliding down to the floor. He pulled his knees to his chest, his hands shaking violently as the adrenaline began to recede, leaving behind a cold, hollow terror. 

Katia knelt beside him, her face pale. She didn't ask if he was okay. She knew he wasn't. 

Maris leaned against the reception desk, chest heaving. "Okay," he breathed. "Okay. We're on a boat. We're going to Sardinia. Does anyone want to tell me what the hell we just ran from?"

Geronimo reached into his jacket and pulled out the book. The leather was completely dry. 

"My past," Geronimo whispered. "And I think... I think it wants to drag us all back into the dark."

Beneath them, the ferry shuddered as the thrusters engaged. The journey to the labyrinth had begun.
