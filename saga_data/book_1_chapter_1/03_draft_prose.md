# BOOK I, CHAPTER 1: THE FIRST AWAKENING

The frost in El Raval did not arrive as a drop in temperature. It arrived as an erasure of memory.

It was a suffocating July night in Barcelona. The heavy, wet Mediterranean heat pressed against the labyrinthine streets of the old port like a damp wool blanket. The air smelled of stale beer, sea salt, and the distant, metallic tang of the freight yards. Yet, inside the ruined back office of Elara’s antiquarian bookshop, Geronimo’s breath plumed in the air. 

He sat on the edge of an overturned mahogany desk, his broad shoulders hunched. The shop had been ransacked three days ago. Books were shredded, floorboards pried upward like broken teeth. Elara was gone, leaving behind only blood and cryptic ledgers. The rest of the team—Maris, Veerle, Inga, and André—were scattered across Europe, chasing phantom logistical leads and dead drops that the enemy had deliberately seeded to separate them. Geronimo and Katia were the vanguard, totally isolated, holding the only genuine piece of the puzzle. 

It wasn't the Obsidian Eye. They hadn't earned that yet. It was merely a splinter. 

Geronimo stared at the cheap glass of water resting on the surviving corner of the desk. A minute ago, the water had been tepid. Now, it was frozen solid. But the air in the room wasn't cold. 

He reached out and struck a wooden match from a box in his pocket. The sulfur flared, a tiny orange beacon. He held it close to the black shard resting on the leather cloth. 

The flame didn't blow out. It froze. The chemical combustion halted mid-burn, the orange fire petrifying into a rigid, fragile sculpture of light, before turning gray and crumbling to ash. Geronimo felt a sudden, terrifying void in his mind—he could no longer remember what the heat of a fire felt like. The splinter was deleting the history of warmth.

"It's not an entropy sink," Katia said from the doorway. 

She stepped into the dim room, her boots crunching over shredded parchment. She was dressed in dark tactical canvas, the sleeves rolled up to reveal the dense, soot-inked protective tattoos winding up her forearms. Her face was pale, tight with a pragmatic dread she rarely allowed to surface. 

"It's a lens," Geronimo murmured, wrapping the thick, cured leather cloth around the splinter. Even through the hide, the erasure bit into his hand, making his metacarpal bones feel as though they had been buried in permafrost for a century. "The true Eye is still on the island. Somewhere under Monte d'Accoddi. This fragment... it’s trying to sync with it. It’s pulling."

"The Grimaldi ferry leaves Port Vell in an hour," Katia said, refusing to look directly at the leather bundle. "If customs checks our bags and finds a sliver of rock that gives them localized amnesia, we’re not getting across the Balearic."

"I've lined the lockbox with lead weaving and rock salt," Geronimo said, standing up. The weight of his pack settled against his spine. "It will dampen the resonance. But we need to move. If the others don't find the secondary nodes, we are walking onto that island blind."

They navigated the humid, shadowed alleys of El Raval in silence. The architecture was a dense collision of gothic stone and modern decay, oblivious to the ghosts that walked its streets. The passenger terminal was a cavernous, chaotic mix of exhausted tourists, screaming children, and freight drivers drinking espresso out of paper cups. 

Security was a cursory wave through metal detectors. The guards were too tired, the line too long, and the Mediterranean summer too oppressive to notice the subtle, icy void radiating from Geronimo’s duffel bag. 

Once aboard the massive vessel, the environment shifted entirely. The rhythmic thrum of the ferry's massive diesel engines vibrated through the steel deck. It was an industrial heartbeat, a promise of distance. Geronimo and Katia secured a utilitarian cabin in the bowels of the ship, dropping their gear onto the thin mattresses. 

As the ferry detached from the Spanish coast, churning the dark water of the Balearic Sea, Geronimo knelt and cracked the lockbox open. 

The frost was gone. The metal was room temperature. 

"The continental shelf," Katia observed, sitting on the edge of her bunk. She was meticulously inspecting a rusted, heavily pitted iron sickle. A *faciola*. The inner curve of the ancient blade was completely dulled, flat, and totally toothless. "We've broken connection with the mainland. The splinter is in transit. Neutral territory."

"Where did you get that?" Geronimo asked, eyeing the rusted farm implement. It clashed violently with her modern tactical gear. 

"Marseille. A black-market archivist specializing in Campidanese witchcraft trials," Katia replied, her tone clinical, betraying none of the tension pooling in her shoulders. "It’s from Villacidro. *Sa Bidda De Is Cogas*. The witches' village."

"We're docking in Porto Torres. That's the extreme north. Villacidro is deep south."

"Folklore isn't bound by municipal borders, Geronimo. Magic migrates when it's hungry. The true Eye is under Monte d'Accoddi, broadcasting a massive, vital energy signature. It's a dinner bell. And the things that answer that bell—the Cogas, the *bruxas*—are hematophagic. They hunt for identity and blood."

She ran her thumb over the blunt edge of the sickle. "It’s a mathematical lock. The Cogas are bound by rigid mythological laws. If you present them with a sickle, they are compelled to count the teeth on the blade. But their minds operate strictly on base-seven logic. They count to seven, lose their place, and are forced to start over. It traps them in an infinite cognitive loop. Because this blade is toothless, it creates a paradox. It should paralyze them entirely."

Geronimo nodded slowly, looking at the closed lockbox. "If the splinter remains neutral."

It did not.

* * *

Twelve hours later, the deep blue of the open Mediterranean gave way to the choppier, emerald currents of the Gulf of Asinara. They were approaching the northern coast of Sardinia. 

Geronimo was jolted awake not by a sound, but by a profound absence. 

He opened his eyes in the cabin. The rhythmic, comforting vibration of the ferry's engines had vanished from his perception. The ambient warmth of the small room was gone. The lockbox on the desk wasn't smoking or glowing red—it was perfectly still, but the steel was covered in a thick layer of rime ice. 

He scrambled out of the bunk. Katia was already standing by the reinforced glass of the porthole, her posture rigid. 

"They're here," she whispered. 

Geronimo grabbed his knife. "Outside? Swarms?"

"No," Katia said, turning slowly. Her face was ashen. "Inside."

Geronimo stepped to the porthole and looked out. The rugged, sun-bleached coast of Sardinia was visible on the horizon, majestic and severe. But the terror wasn't in the sky. It was on the promenade deck just below them. 

A middle-aged businessman in a crumpled linen suit was leaning against the railing, smoking a cigarette. As Geronimo watched, the man's left eye flickered. The brown iris dissolved, swirling into a stark, predatory yellow. The man blinked, and his jawline audibly cracked, elongating, shifting into something entirely unfamiliar. 

The man didn't seem to notice. The woman standing next to him didn't notice. But the man was being replaced. 

"They don't fly across the water," Katia breathed, horrified realization dawning on her. "They ride the psychic wake of the passengers. They replace them. One remembered feature at a time. They are stealing their identities to cross the sea."

As if hearing her, the businessman turned his head. His yellow eye snapped directly upward, locking onto the cabin porthole. The cigarette dropped from his hand. At the base of his spine, bulging visibly beneath the fabric of the linen suit, a grotesque, fleshy protrusion twitched. A vestigial tail. 

The man smiled—a wide, unnatural stretching of flesh—and broke into a dead sprint toward the interior stairwell. 

"We need to move. Now," Geronimo barked, throwing his canvas jacket on and grabbing the lockbox. The cold burning through the handle was agonizing. 

They burst out of the cabin into the narrow, fluorescent-lit corridor. The ship's intercom crackled to life, the captain cheerfully announcing their final approach into Porto Torres. 

Passengers were crowding the hallways, dragging luggage. To Geronimo’s horror, the infection was spreading. A teenager’s hair turned stark white. An old woman’s fingers elongated into multi-jointed, spider-like appendages. The Cogas were bleeding into reality, using the passengers as physical clay. 

The businessman from the deck rounded the corner at the end of the hall. He was no longer entirely human. His arms hung too low, his jaw unhinged. 

Katia stepped in front of Geronimo, raising the rusted iron sickle from Villacidro high into the air. 

The shifting entity froze, its yellow eyes locking instantly onto the curved blade. The compulsion took hold. Its jaw clicked as it began to process the command. 

"Uno," Katia commanded in Logudorese. 

The entity twitched. But it did not freeze in a loop. 

Instead, as Katia spoke the number, the businessman's shifting, unstable face snapped into sharp, terrifying focus. The flesh solidified. The yellow eyes hardened. 

"Due," Katia said, her voice faltering as she backed away. 

The entity's shoulders broadened. The vestigial tail beneath its suit whipped violently. It took a heavy, solid step forward. 

Geronimo realized the horror of the trap in an instant. The proximity of the true Eye beneath Monte d'Accoddi had inverted the protective law. The splinter in Geronimo's hand was acting as a warped lens. The Cogas were entities of stolen identity—by counting them, by assigning them numerical value in the physical world, Katia wasn't trapping them. She was giving them mass. She was giving them identity. 

The entity lunged. 

Katia swung the sickle, burying the blunt iron into the creature's collarbone. It didn't bleed. It merely grinned, reaching out to grasp her throat. 

Suddenly, the heavy maritime safety netting strung along the corridor bulkhead shifted. The thick, hempen ropes, woven to prevent passengers from falling against the steel walls during rough seas, writhed as if alive. 

The knots untied and retied themselves in a fraction of a second. A complex, impossible geometry formed in the hemp. It wasn't a mechanical failure. A presence was residing in the weave. 

A Jana. 

The ancient Sardinian weavers of fate, disturbed by the massive temporal anomaly of the splinter, were already embedded in the island's approaching threshold. The rope lashed out, wrapping around the Coga’s throat and wrists. The Jana did not fight with brute force; it fought with geometry. The knots tightened in patterns that defied three-dimensional space, creating a physical paradox that the base-seven mind of the Coga could not process. 

The entity screamed—a dual-toned shriek of shifting vocal cords—as it was dragged backward into the bulkhead, completely entangled in a knot that had no beginning and no end. 

They bolted down the stairwell toward the vehicle deck. The air was choked with diesel exhaust and the panicked shouts of drivers. The Cogas were fully dropping the facade now. Half a dozen passengers in the hold were contorting, their bones snapping and reforming, vestigial tails tearing through clothing as they abandoned their human hosts. 

The massive steel ramp at the front of the ferry began to lower with a deafening hydraulic groan, grinding against the thick concrete of the pier. The bright Mediterranean sun flooded into the dark hold. They were docking at Molo Ponente. 

But as Geronimo and Katia sprinted down the ramp, the reality of the port began to fracture. 

The proximity of the splinter to the true Eye was causing a temporal overlap. The modern concrete of Molo Ponente flickered like a dying television screen. Pushing through the asphalt, phantom and translucent but undeniably real, rose the ancient, sun-bleached columns of Roman *Turris Libisonis*. The ghosts of the ancient port were occupying the same physical space as the modern harbor. 

Looming immediately to their right, anchoring the temporal storm, was the Torre Aragonese. 

The massive, 14th-century stone watchtower cast a long, dark shadow over the docks. Behind them, the contorted, fully-realized Cogas poured down the ramp, their yellow eyes fixed on the lockbox in Geronimo's hand. 

Geronimo’s mind raced through Katia's lore as the ancient Roman mosaic tiles flickered beneath his boots. Their counting limit. Their perception was locked to base seven.

He looked at the Torre Aragonese. 

"The tower," Geronimo gasped, hauling Katia toward the massive stone structure. "It's octagonal. It has eight sides."

They threw themselves into the deep shadow of the tower, pressing their backs against the rough, sun-baked stone where two of the eight walls met in a sharp angle. 

The pack of Cogas halted at the edge of the shadow. 

They shrieked, their heads darting wildly, sniffing the air. But they did not step forward. They paced the perimeter of the tower's shadow, confused, their perception fracturing. The geometric reality of an eight-sided structure could not be processed by minds bound by a hard limit of seven. The eighth angle created a cognitive dead zone. To the Cogas, the shadow of the Torre Aragonese simply did not exist. Geronimo and Katia had vanished into a mathematical void. 

Slowly, frustrated, the entities turned away, blending back into the panicked crowds of disembarking passengers, stealing new faces, vanishing into the modern city of Porto Torres. 

Geronimo slid down the rough stone wall, his lungs burning. He opened the lockbox, his hands trembling. 

The obsidian splinter inside was entirely inert. It wasn't cold. It wasn't hot. It was just a piece of black glass. 

He looked south, past the modern industrial cranes, past the phantom Roman columns, toward the rolling hills where the ziggurat of Monte d'Accoddi lay buried. 

It wasn't a beacon, Geronimo realized, the true horror of the artifact finally settling into his bones. The splinter hadn't been guiding them. 

The true Obsidian Eye, buried miles away in the dark, had simply used the shard as a lens. It had looked across the sea. It had seen the ferry, the Cogas, the Janas in the weave. 

And it had looked right through him.
