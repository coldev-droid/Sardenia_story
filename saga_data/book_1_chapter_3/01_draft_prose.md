The grey shadow of Capo Caccia stretched across the Golfo di Alghero like a sleeping leviathan as the *Sentina* limped toward the harbor breakwater. The yacht’s auxiliary engine hummed on a reduced three-phase loop; the electrical pulse from the previous night’s encounter had fried the main port bus bar and scorched the secondary telemetry arrays.

Geronimo stood at the bow, holding the cold brass railing with both hands. His palm pressed against his jacket pocket where the lead box rested, heavy and inert. Yet every few minutes, a phantom wave of cold radiated through his ribcage, leaving behind a blank space in his immediate memory. He remembered entering the bay; he did not remember turning past the outer cardinal buoy.

"You're drifting again, Geronimo," Katia said softly, stepping up beside him. Her fingers were stained with indigo dye from handling the Bisso weave. "The obsidian is taking the edges off your short-term recall. If we leave it unshielded much longer, the thermal amnesia will reach your procedural memory."

"I know how to steer a boat," Geronimo grunted, though his eyes struggled to focus on the yellow light of the harbor entrance. "We dock, we fix the bus bar, we trace the conduit."

By 06:00, the *Sentina* was tied to the quay at the Porto di Alghero, under the high yellow limestone walls of the Catalan bastions. The morning air carried the smell of diesel, salt, and damp stone. On the stone pier, an elderly fisherman in a faded woolen cap was mending a trammel net. He watched their burnt antenna mast with quiet suspicion.

When André leaned over the gunwale to toss a spring line, the old man spoke without looking up. "You come from the south side of the cape?"

"We came across the gulf," André replied in Italian.

"Don't go near the water caves at the point today," the fisherman said, pointing a gnarled finger toward the sheer cliffs of Capo Caccia five miles to the west. "The sea isn't running high, but the swell inside the rock is beating like a drum. It’s making the water in the harbor basins rise and fall six inches every minute. S'Urtzu is thrashing in the limestone."

Maris came up from the engine room, wiping greased knuckles with a rag. She overheard the fisherman and exchanged a glance with Katia. 

"The swell isn't meteorological," Maris said, lowering her voice as the team gathered around the navigation table in the wheelhouse. "The harbor tide gauges are showing localized harmonic spikes. We split the team here. We can't take the *Sentina* anywhere near Capo Caccia until we fix the power distribution and reinforce the lead box."

Maris laid out the strategic split on the chart.

"Team B takes the yacht and the shore duty," Maris instructed. "Inga, André, and I stay at the marina. Inga, you recalibrate the bridge sensors and isolate the fried relays. André and I will walk into L'Alguer—the old town—to source dense metallurgical lead or raw galena from the old maritime foundries near the port wall. We need to double-hull the containment box for the Obsidian Splinter before the cold spreads further into Geronimo's mind. Mia and Tina will keep watch on the perimeter. The Cogas we drove off last night haven't disappeared; they're waiting for the vessel to sit dark."

"And Team A?" asked Katia.

"Team A takes the rubber tender," Maris said, looking at Geronimo and Veerle. "Geronimo, Katia, Veerle. You take the Bisso fragment and head directly across the gulf to Capo Caccia. The Janna conduit is tied to the water level at Grotta di Nettuno. But watch the stone. If the myth has awakened as an acoustic wave inside the caves, the limestone will turn into a speaker."

***

By mid-morning, the division was absolute. 

In the narrow, cobblestoned lanes of Alghero's historic center—beneath Spanish Gothic archways and dry laundry strung between stone facades—Maris and André searched for high-density shielding. The street grid of L'Alguer felt compressed, its medieval defensive walls insulating the interior from the sea breeze. 

"The dockyard brass worker near Via Doria has old keel weights," André murmured, keeping his eyes on the narrow rooftops above them. The morning sun cast sharp shadows across the limestone masonry. "Sheet lead, ninety-nine percent pure."

"Get two sheets," Maris said. She paused at a street corner, her hand dropping to the voltage meter in her pocket. The needle twitched violently toward the red line, then settled back to zero. "Look up."

On the tiled roof of an abandoned chapel forty yards away, three sea gulls sat motionless in a perfect, equidistant line. They were not preening or watching the port for fish. Their black eyes were fixed entirely on the *Sentina* docked half a mile away, where Inga was crouched over the exposed main electrical panel.

"Cogas," André whispered, his hand tightening around the heavy spanner in his coat. "They're monitoring our grounding."

"Let them watch," Maris said grimly. "As long as Inga keeps the vessel's hull isolated from the shore power grid, they can't cross the dock iron. Let's get the lead."

***

Five miles across the Gulf of Alghero, the rigid inflatable tender cut through the deep blue mirror of the open bay. Geronimo held the tiller, his jaw clenched against a throbbing ache behind his temples. Every time the boat bounced over a swell, a brief fog wiped his thoughts—what was Katia’s middle name? What year was the *Sentina* launched? He forced his focus back to the towering white face of Capo Caccia rising three hundred meters out of the water.

In the bow, Katia held a sealed acrylic box containing the Bisso fabric. The sea silk sat motionlessly inside, its golden-brown threads faint and organic. Beside her, Veerle monitored an acoustic hydrophone lowered into the water off the port gunwale.

"We're three hundred yards from the entrance to Grotta di Nettuno," Veerle called over the engine noise. She pulled off her headphones, her face pale. "Geronimo, slow us down. The hydrophone isn't picking up water movement. It’s picking up a standing wave."

Geronimo pulled back the throttle. The outboard engine sputtered to an idle. 

The cliff face of Capo Caccia towered directly above them, a massive wall of Cretaceous limestone carved with sea caves at the water line. The main mouth of the Grotta di Nettuno lay just ahead, where the sea lapped gently against the lowest step of the Escala del Cabirol—the massive cliffside staircase carved into the rock face.

There was no wind. The surface of the sea was smooth as glass. Yet, inside the dark cave mouth, the water was churning violently, sloshing against the limestone walls in a rhythmic, mechanical thrust.

"It's not the wind," Katia said, unlatching the acrylic case. She lifted the damp Bisso fragment with gloved hands. "Look at the fabric."

The golden-brown sea silk began to vibrate. The fine fibers lifted into the air, stiffening like iron needles, pointing directly toward the mouth of the grotto.

Instantly, a deep, sub-audible shudder passed through the tender's hull. 

It was not a sound heard through the ears; it was a low-frequency acoustic resonance that struck directly at the sternum. The sea cave acted as a giant Helmholtz resonator. The air pressure inside the karst chamber oscillated with sudden, terrifying force—a roaring, rhythmic thud that sounded like a massive, wild beast thrashing against the hollow roots of the island.

*S'Urtzu.*

"My head—" Geronimo gasped, dropping to one knee as his hands flew to his ears. The acoustic vibration struck the lead box in his pocket, sympathetic resonance magnifying the obsidian's field. The horizon rolled sideways. For three seconds, his mind was totally blanked—he forgot his own name, forgot the boat, forgot the ocean.

"Geronimo! Hold the tiller!" Katia screamed, her voice barely audible over the roaring air pressure coming from the cave. The air around the tender grew thick, smelling of crushed bone and wet horn.

Digital displays on the tender’s GPS unit shattered. The glass screens cracked in spiderweb patterns under the brutal sonic force. 

Veerle reached forward, grabbing the Bisso from Katia’s hands, and thrust it toward the dark mouth of the cavern. As the sacred silk met the acoustic jet streaming from the grotto, the violent resonance suddenly changed pitch—shifting from a chaotic, destructive roar into a clear, terrifying harmonic note that echoed off the high cliffs of Capo Caccia.
