Here, in the limestone heart of Sardinia, the world had been stripped of its softness. Every surface was a fractal of sharp edges and sudden voids, a labyrinth of pale grey and blinding ivory that mirrored the bleached bones of ancient ungulates scattered across the scrub. Geronimo led the procession, his pace measured by a rhythmic, internal metronome, his boots striking the stone with a precision that avoided the treacherous, hollow crusts of the sinkholes. Behind him, the team followed in a disciplined line, their shadows condensed into tight, ink-black pools beneath their feet, as if the noon heat had compressed the very dimension of darkness.

The air was thick with the scent of crushed mastic and wild thyme, a pungent, resinous perfume that clung to the back of the throat, mixing with the alkaline dust raised by their passage. This dust, a fine powder of calcium carbonate, settled in the creases of their clothing and the pores of their skin, turning them into ghosts of the mountain. Katia walked second, her eyes scanning the horizon with a cartographer’s rigor. She noted the way the limestone ridges folded into one another, creating a series of concentric arcs that suggested a massive, prehistoric collapse. To her, the landscape was not a wilderness but a ruined cathedral of geology, where the pillars had been dissolved by eons of acidic rain and the floors had fallen away into the subterranean dark.

Maris followed, her focus internalized, her mind calculating the atmospheric pressure and the slow, agonizing shift in humidity as they descended toward the basin. She felt the heat as a physical weight, a thermal blanket that dampened the sound of their breathing. The silence of the Supramonte was not an absence of noise but a presence—a heavy, humming vibration produced by millions of cicadas, whose rhythmic shrieking formed a wall of sound that seemed to vibrate in the very marrow of their bones. It was a sonic architecture that defined the boundaries of their movement, a shimmering veil of noise that blurred the edges of reality.

Further back, Veerle and Inga moved in a synchronized duality, their strides mirroring one another, their eyes attuned to the subtle shifts in the rock’s coloration. Inga, whose perception was calibrated to the smallest anomalies, noticed the way the grey limestone occasionally gave way to streaks of deep, oxidised red—the signature of iron deposits leaching through the strata. These veins were like arterial sprays across the pale stone, marking the path of ancient watercourses that had long since vanished into the deep aquifers. Veerle, meanwhile, watched the perimeter, her awareness extending outward in a radius of careful apprehension. She was attuned to the wind, which arrived in sudden, erratic gusts, carrying the scent of the distant sea and the dry, metallic tang of sun-baked minerals.

André brought up the rear, the anchor of the group, his movements slow and deliberate. He carried the heavier gear, the weight of the equipment shifting with a mechanical cadence against his spine. He observed the team not as individuals, but as a singular organism moving through a hostile medium. He noted the precise distance between Geronimo and Katia—exactly two meters—and the way the line contracted and expanded as the terrain dictated. For André, the journey was a series of vectors and angles, a problem of logistics being solved in real-time across a surface of unpredictable friction.

As they rounded a jagged promontory, the landscape opened into a hidden valley, a natural amphitheatre of white stone that seemed to breathe with a cool, subterranean respiration. In the center of this basin sat a *domus de janas*—a house of the fairies. It was not a building in the traditional sense, but a series of chambers carved directly into the living rock, the entrance a narrow, rectangular aperture that looked like a wound in the cliffside. The architecture was primitive yet precise, the walls smoothed by tools that had been lost to the Neolithic fog. According to the folklore that Geronimo had whispered during their ascent, these were the dwellings of the *Janas*, the immortal weavers who spun gold thread in the silence of the earth, their presence marked by the scent of honey and the sound of distant, phantom looms.

Geronimo stopped. The movement was sudden, a complete cessation of momentum that rippled back through the line. He stood at the threshold of the cave, his silhouette framed by the blinding light of the plateau. He did not enter immediately; instead, he waited, allowing the equilibrium of the group to settle. He was listening to the silence of the *domus*, searching for the subtle shift in air currents that would indicate the depth of the interior.

"The geometry is wrong," Katia whispered, stepping up beside him. She was looking at the orientation of the entrance. "It doesn’t align with the solar solstice, nor the lunar nadir. It’s oriented toward something internal."

Maris joined them, her hand brushing the cool surface of the limestone. The rock here was different; it was damp, the humidity rising in a visible shimmer from the dark maw of the cave. "The temperature drop is exactly seven degrees Celsius over a distance of three meters," she noted, her voice flat and analytical. "There is a significant thermal exchange occurring. The cave is breathing."

The transition from the blinding exterior to the interior was a sensory collapse. As they stepped inside, the world shifted from a palette of white and cobalt to one of deep ochre, charcoal, and obsidian. The light, filtered through the narrow entrance, stretched into long, cinematic shafts that illuminated the dancing dust motes in precise, linear columns. The walls of the *domus* were not merely carved; they were textured. Small, rhythmic indentations ran along the ceiling, a sequence of pits that resembled a binary code etched into the stone.

Inga moved closer to the wall, her fingers tracing the patterns. "These aren't accidental," she murmured. "Look at the spacing. They are equidistant. Every four centimeters, a depression. It’s a measurement."

The interior of the fairy house opened into a central chamber, a perfect oval that felt like the belly of a great stone beast. In the center of the room, a single slab of basalt—black, dense, and foreign to the surrounding limestone—lay embedded in the floor. The basalt was polished to a mirror sheen, reflecting the distorted images of the six adventurers above it. The contrast was jarring: the pale, porous limestone of the walls surrounding a core of volcanic darkness.

Veerle stepped back, her instincts firing. "There is a void beneath the basalt," she said, her voice echoing in the oval chamber. "The acoustics are shifting. The sound of our breathing is being amplified, but the floor... the floor is absorbing the frequency."

André knelt by the edge of the slab, producing a small ultrasonic sensor from his pack. He placed the device on the black stone, the screen flickering with rapid, oscillating waves. "The density is inconsistent," he reported. "There is a cavity approximately twelve meters below us. The volume is roughly cubic, with walls that are perfectly orthogonal. This isn't a natural cavern. It's a construction."

The realization hung in the air, a heavy, intellectual weight. They were standing atop a hidden architecture, a nested secret within a secret. Geronimo looked up at the ceiling, where the *Janas* were said to have woven their gold. He saw the way the shadows pooled in the corners of the room, creating the illusion of figures standing just out of sight. The folklore of Sardinia was not merely a collection of myths; it was a linguistic map, a way of describing geological and architectural anomalies through the lens of the supernatural. The "gold thread" of the fairies was likely a metaphor for the veins of quartz or perhaps a more complex, forgotten technology that had once pulsed through the mountain.

"We are not the first to find the alignment," Geronimo said, his voice resonating in the oval space. "The *Janas* didn't build this. They were the guardians of it. The weavers were keepers of the threshold."

Katia began to sketch the layout of the room in her notebook, her pencil moving in rapid, precise strokes. She noted the relationship between the basalt slab and the entrance, the angle of the shadows, and the sequence of the wall indentations. As she worked, she felt a strange sensation of being watched—not by eyes, but by the space itself. The room felt sentient, a machine of stone and silence that had been waiting for a specific set of variables to be met before it would reveal its purpose.

The team settled into a cautious perimeter, their gear laid out in a symmetrical arrangement around the basalt slab. The slow pacing of their movements was a necessity; in a space this attuned to resonance, a sudden gesture could feel like a violation. They spoke in low tones, their voices stripped of urgency, replaced by a clinical, shared focus.

"If the cavity below is orthogonal," Maris hypothesized, "then we are dealing with a structure that defies the natural erosion patterns of the Supramonte. Limestone dissolves in curves, in spheres, in jagged teeth. Right angles are the signature of intent."

"Intent from whom?" Inga asked, her gaze fixed on the dark reflection in the stone.

"The ones who knew how to carve basalt with the precision of a diamond," André replied, his eyes still on the sensor's readout. "The ones who understood that the limestone plateau was the perfect camouflage—a porous, chaotic shell to hide a rigid, mathematical core."

As they debated, the light from the entrance began to shift. The sun, moving in its celestial arc, reached a point where a single, needle-thin beam of light pierced through a hidden fissure in the ceiling. The beam descended with agonizing slowness, a golden thread cutting through the gloom. It traveled across the ochre walls, ignoring the carvings, ignoring the dust, until it struck the center of the basalt slab.

The moment the light touched the black stone, the reflection changed. The mirror-like surface did not merely reflect the light; it absorbed it, the gold beam vanishing into the depths of the basalt. For a heartbeat, there was absolute silence. Then, a low-frequency hum began to emanate from beneath their feet, a vibration that started in the soles of their boots and climbed upward through their skeletons. It was a mathematical sound, a pure tone that matched the frequency of the cicadas outside, creating a sudden, violent resonance that made the walls of the *domus* seem to shimmer.

The basalt slab did not move, but the air above it began to ripple, the thermal layers distorting into a series of concentric circles. Geronimo stepped forward, his hand outstretched, his fingers hovering just millimeters above the vibrating stone. He could feel the heat now—not the oppressive heat of the Sardinian sun, but a focused, electromagnetic warmth that smelled of ozone and ancient electricity.

"The sequence is starting," he whispered.

The team closed in, their positions forming a perfect hexagon around the slab, their breathing synchronized by the pulsing hum of the mountain. They were no longer just explorers; they had become part of the circuit, the final components required to activate a mechanism that had slept for millennia beneath the white silence of the Supramonte. The deep sensory details of the room—the smell of the damp stone, the vibration in their teeth, the singular golden thread of light—merged into a single, overwhelming experience of anticipation. They stood on the precipice of a descent, not just into the earth, but into a logic that preceded history.

Geronimo’s fingertips finally made contact. The moment his skin touched the basalt, the resonance ceased instantly, replaced by a silence so absolute it felt like a physical weight pressing against their eardrums. For a heartbeat, the world vanished—the golden thread of light, the damp walls, the faces of his companions—leaving him suspended in a void of pure, humming consciousness. Then, the slab didn’t move; it dissolved. 

The solid stone transitioned into a state of liquid obsidian, swirling in slow, hypnotic vortices that defied gravity. It was not melting from heat, but shifting its molecular frequency. The hexagon of people, still linked by the unseen current, felt a sudden, irresistible pull. The vacuum created by the stone’s transformation acted like a siphon, drawing them inward.

"Don't break the circle!" Elena shouted, her voice sounding distant, as if she were speaking from the bottom of a deep well. 

They stepped forward in unison, their boots clicking one last time on the cave floor before they crossed the threshold. The sensation was not one of falling, but of being folded. Space compressed; the air grew thick and sweet, tasting of ancient rain and crushed mint. They were sliding down a throat of shimmering glass, a vertical conduit that bypassed the geological layers of the Supramonte. Around them, the walls of the shaft were etched with glyphs that flickered in and out of existence—mathematical constants, celestial alignments, and biological blueprints that seemed to breathe in time with their own heartbeats.

When the motion finally stopped, the transition was as abrupt as the start. They landed softly on a floor of translucent quartz, the impact dampened by an unseen kinetic field. 

Geronimo gasped, his lungs expanding with air that felt hyper-oxygenated, invigorating and sharp. He looked up and felt a vertigo that had nothing to do with height. They were in a vaulted chamber of impossible proportions, a subterranean cathedral where the pillars were not carved from stone, but grown from a fusion of organic crystal and conductive metal. The ceiling was a mirrored dome, reflecting a star-map that did not correspond to any modern sky.

In the heart of the chamber stood a spire of rotating rings, each one spinning on a different axis. The rings were composed of a material that seemed to swallow the light, shifting from deep violet to a blinding silver. As they approached, the spire reacted to their presence. The rotations slowed, the rings aligning with a series of metallic clicks that sounded like the locking of a cosmic vault.

Geronimo felt a pull in his chest, a magnetic tether drawing him toward the spire. As he walked, the quartz floor beneath his feet began to illuminate, tracing paths of gold light that connected the spire to twelve smaller pedestals arranged in a wide circle.

"The logic," Geronimo murmured, his eyes fixed on the rotating rings. "It’s not based on linear time. This place... it exists in the intervals. The spaces between seconds."

"What are you talking about, Geronimo?" Elena asked, catching up to him, her scientific mind struggling to categorize the impossible.

We had to match the frequency of this place to be seen by it. We didn't descend into the earth; we shifted into a different phase of the same location."

It was the source of the ozone smell, the origin of the hum that had haunted his dreams since he first arrived in Sardinia.

As Geronimo stared into the shard, images began to flood his mind—not as memories, but as data streams. He saw the Supramonte not as a landscape of limestone and scrub, but as a living organism, a biological antenna designed to capture signals from the galactic core. "For the Return," Geronimo replied.

Suddenly, the spire flared. The rings accelerated, spinning so fast they became a blurred sphere of silver. A voice—or the impression of a voice—echoed not in the air, but directly in their minds. It was a harmonic chord, a layering of a thousand tones that conveyed a sense of profound antiquity and an aching, timeless loneliness.

*The Circuit is Closed.*

The golden paths on the floor surged with power, the light rushing from the pedestals toward the central spire. The team was thrown backward by a wave of static electricity, their hair standing on end, their skin tingling. 

Elena scrambled to her feet, her eyes wide. "Something is happening. Look at the star-map!"

Above them, the mirrored dome was shifting. The alien constellations were rearranging themselves, sliding across the ceiling like pieces of a giant puzzle. The stars coalesced into a single, blinding point of light that projected a beam straight down through the center of the spire, striking the iridescent shard.

They slammed back onto the basalt slab in the *domus*.

The basalt slab was solid once more, its surface dull and lifeless, as if it had never been anything more than a piece of volcanic rock.

Elena stood up, her expression a mix of terror and exhilaration. "If the Silence is ending, if those signals are returning... what does that mean for us? For the world?"

Geronimo looked up at the ceiling of the *domus*, seeing through the stone, through the mountain, and into the vast, unfolding tapestry of the cosmos. He could feel the other sites now—hidden nodes in the earth, sleeping giants in the ice of Antarctica, drowned spires in the depths of the Pacific. They were all waking up, triggered by the completion of the circuit in Sardinia.

To any observer, they were just three exhausted explorers emerging from a hole in the ground. But as Geronimo looked toward the horizon, he could see the air shimmering, the thermal layers distorting into concentric circles, mirroring the pattern of the basalt slab.

The world looked the same, but the logic had changed. The game had shifted from discovery to survival. 

"Come on," Geronimo called back to the others, his voice echoing across the white silence of the Supramonte. "We have work to do. The others will be waking up soon."
