The descent along the eastern flank of the Supramonte limestone plateau did not yield to speed, nor did it permit the illusion of direct progression. Here, where the Jurassic karst had been sheared by ancient tectonic subsidence into a sequence of stair-stepped escarpments, every vertical meter was contested by the friable nature of the rock. The stone was a pale, iron-stained dolostone, pitted with micro-cavities that dissolved under the pressure of a gloved fingertip into fine grey flour. Above them, the canopy of dwarf holm oak and gnarled *arbutus* had long since given way to stark, sun-bleached ramparts; below, the throat of the sinkhole—a *supramonte* sinkhole locally remembered in the dialects of Baunei as a *dolina* of the forgotten weavers—swallowed the mid-afternoon light in a smooth gradient from ochre to slate blue.

Geronimo stopped three paces short of the ledge where the limestone met an exposed lens of darker, older Permian trachyte. His left hand, anchored against a calcified overhang, felt the precise vibration of the wind shearing through the lower gorge: a low, resonant hum attuned to twenty-two cycles per second, the acoustic signature of a cavernous interior extending hundreds of meters back toward the central massif. He unclipped the survey clinometer from his chest harness, adjusted the brass thumb-screw until the fluid-damped dial stabilized, and read the angle.

"Thirty-seven degrees downward slope along the fault line," Geronimo said, his voice flat, dropped an octave to prevent the sound from echoing into the gorge before them. "The trachyte layer acts as an aquiclude. The water followed the contact zone, cutting a smooth conduit under the limestone cap. If the survey from 1894 is accurate to within two meters, the entrance to the lower gallery sits precisely eighty-four meters along this bearing."

Behind him, André cleared a patch of damp leaf-mould from the rock interface with the steel edge of his folding spade. His notebook lay open on his thigh, the rag-paper pages already damp from the high relative humidity wafting up from the abyss. With a 0.3-millimeter graphite lead, he drafted the profile of the escarpment, marking the structural dip with a series of parallel hatchings. 

"The bedding planes are tilted seven degrees to the south-southeast," André noted, without looking up from his sketch. "Look at the drag folds in the shale band just above the trachyte. The stone didn't merely fracture during the Oligocene rifting; it sheared with a rotational motion. That means any ancient masonry built inside the natural fault won't be level to the horizon. It will be keyed into the dip of the mountain itself. If we look for plumb lines, we will miss the passages entirely."

Katia stood on the narrow lip of the shelf, her weight distributed evenly between the balls of her boots, her body turned at a twenty-degree angle to the wall to minimize her profile against the open drop. Her right hand rested lightly on the woven nylon sheath of her primary anchor pin. Her eyes scanned the shadow line sixty meters below, where the white limestone vanished into the subterranean shadow. Her training did not permit her to look at the scenery as a landscape; to her, it was a sequence of fall lines, loose scree channels, cover points, and acoustic dead-zones.

"Movement in the shadows is nonexistent, but the air current is pulsing," Katia said softly, her breath steady, unhurried. "Every twelve seconds, there is a pressure differential. The sinkhole is breathing out. That means there is a secondary opening at a higher elevation, likely on the northern ridge near the *cuile* ruins, creating a natural stack effect. The air coming out smells of damp clay, old iron oxides, and dry goat dung. It's clean air, but it’s deep."

"It is the air of the *Domus*," Veerle murmured from the shadow of a wild olive root that clung precariously to the fracture zone. She was unrolling a linen wrap containing three brass-bound geological maps and a set of hand-drawn transcriptions taken from nineteenth-century parish registers in Oliena. "The shepherd oral histories collected by Ferraro in 1912 spoke of this precise *dolina*. They called it *S'Mera de sas Janas*—the thread of the fairy weavers. They believed that inside these trachyte-and-limestone conduits, women of small stature sat at looms made of basalt, weaving golden thread that was endless. But the gold was not metal; it was the yellow iron-sulfide silt deposited by the seasonal floods through the pyrite veins."

"Pyrite means sulfuric acid weathering," Inga observed, leaning her heavy pack against the wall with a dry, metallic clatter of Carabiners and climbing chocks. She took a deep breath of the rising air, her nostrils flaring slightly as her sharp mind cataloged the chemical trace. "Which explains the roundness of the conduit walls. It’s not just water abrasion; it’s chemical solution along the bedding planes. If the acidity was high enough to rot the limestone into smooth tubes, it would have softened any soft stone tools or unsealed wood left down there within three centuries. Only the hard basalt and the high-density bronze will have survived."

Maris was already at work on the anchor assembly. She chose two deep, water-carved pockets in the dense trachyte—pockets forged by thousands of years of swirling pebbles during ancient flash floods—and inserted two expanding mechanical bolts. The sound of her ratcheting wrench was crisp and rhythmic: four short clicks, a pause to test the torque with her palm, four short clicks again. The steel of the hangers glinted dark against the reddish-brown matrix of the trachyte.

"Anchor one set," Maris reported quietly, her fingers tracing the seam between the mechanical bolt and the living stone. "Tensile capacity exceeds two thousand kilograms. The rock is crystalline, free of micro-fractures in this specific lens. We descend one by one. I’ll lead the line to establish the intermediate re-belay at the forty-meter mark, right where the trachyte enters the deep shadow."

"I'll follow second," Katia said, her eyes already calculating the rope trajectory to ensure no sharp limestone edges would chafe the core during tension cycles. "Geronimo third with the survey kit, then Veerle, André with the spatial mapping gear, and Inga taking the drag to clear the ropes and maintain tail security."

Geronimo nodded once, an economy of motion that characterized his leadership. He reached down, took a pinch of the terra rossa soil from a micro-terrace near his boots, and rubbed it between his index finger and thumb. The clay was cold, rich in hematite, and held a greasy consistency that indicated constant, low-level capillary moisture from the deep rock. 

"Check illuminated instruments," Geronimo commanded. "Set chronometers to local solar time: fourteen hours, twenty-two minutes. We operate under three-point spatial contact at all times down the chute. Veerle, keep your focus on the wall features at the thirty-meter line. Look for structural dressing—places where the natural water-worn curves have been squared off by bronze chisels. The Nuragic masons did not fight the karst; they followed the water, dressing only the passages that allowed them to align their sightlines with the equinox shafts."

Maris dropped the coil of sixty-millimeter static line into the abyss. The rope unreeled with a soft, whistling sound, a long black snake uncurling into the twilight of the gorge before its weighted end struck the bottom ledge forty-meter below with a muted, rubbery *thud*. The sound took 0.12 seconds to return to their ears, giving them an immediate acoustic measurement of the wall density in the upper chamber.

Without a word, Maris clipped her descender into the line, locked the friction safety, stepped over the lip of the shelf, and vanished beneath the horizon of stone. The tension on the line hummed a low *E-flat* note against the static anchor pins.

The descent was a exercise in slow thermal transition. As Katia went over the edge, followed by Geronimo, the ambient air temperature dropped abruptly from twenty-eight degrees Celsius on the sun-lit upper terraces to a constant, marble-cold fourteen degrees inside the sinkhole's throat. The sound of the Mediterranean wind overhead dissolved into the absolute silence of the mountain's interior—a silence that was not an absence of sound, but a dense, heavy presence composed of falling water drops, distant ground-tremors from the distant coast, and the rhythmic sound of their own lungs expanding against harness straps.

At thirty-five meters down, Geronimo paused on a foot-wide shelf of dark basalt. The transition between the upper Mesozoic limestone and the lower Paleozoic basement was as clean as a saw-cut. The white rock above ended in a horizontal seam filled with a two-centimeter layer of green, greasy chlorite schist; below it, the basalt was nearly black, fine-grained, and carved into smooth, concave scoops by ancient hydraulic pressure.

He brought his tactical light to bear on the rock surface five inches from his left eye. 

"Here," Geronimo called up softly. The light grazed the stone at an oblique angle of ten degrees, revealing details invisible under direct light.

Three parallel grooves, each six millimeters wide and four millimeters deep, ran horizontally along the basalt face. They were not natural striae from glacial or hydraulic transport; the interior surfaces of the grooves bore the microscopic step-patterns of a hand-driven point-chisel. Between the middle and lower groove, weathered almost into illegibility by centuries of mineral-laden seepage, was an incised symbol: a circle intersected by two vertical lines, flanked by four tiny, pocked depressions arranged in a precise diamond grid.

Veerle dropped down next to him, her harness carabiner clicking softly against his. She leaned in until her forehead nearly touched the cold basalt, her breath fogging the dark stone for a brief second before the cool draft swept the moisture away.

"The sign of the *Scaia*," she whispered, her voice tinged with the quiet satisfaction of a long-sought proof. "It isn't a boundary mark for a territory. It’s a hydraulic notation. In the folklore of the Marghine range, the old women spoke of the * water that counts its own steps*. The circle represents the cistern head; the two vertical lines are the primary feeder channels cut through the trachyte. And these four points... they aren't decorative. They correspond to the four seasonal water levels before the great collapse in the ninth century BC."

"And the masonry?" André asked, descending to a stop just three feet above them, his body hanging suspended in his harness, pencil held between his teeth as he adjusted his headlamp angle.

"Look below your feet, André," Maris said from her position ten meters further down, her torch beam cutting a sharp wedge through the suspension of microscopic dust particles. "The basalt isn't native to this exact horizon of the fault. It’s been brought here. These are dressed blocks, fitted without mortar, laid in a radial arch that supports the entire ceiling of the lower gallery."

They pushed down another twelve meters, reaching the floor of the primary intake chamber. Here, the floor leveled off into a broad, oval hall sixty meters long and twenty meters wide. The ground was composed of fine, compacted terra rossa mixed with water-worn granite pebbles that must have been carried miles through subterranean streams from the high peaks of the Gennargentu range. 

The atmosphere was thick with the scent of wet iron, ancient lime, and something else—a faint, resinous odor like dry lentisk sap or juniper smoke, preserved in the airtight, cool recesses of the lower chamber for millennia.

Inga reached the floor last, unclipping from the main line with an efficient, double-handed motion that spoke of long practice. She immediately knelt, scooping a handful of the floor sediment into a clear poly-bag for later geochemical testing, then shone her torch down the length of the hall.

"The humidity is eighty-eight percent," Inga reported, glancing at the digital gauge on her wrist. "Carbon dioxide levels are normal—four hundred and twenty parts per million. Air movement is coming from the north-northwest, through that narrow fissure behind the stacked masonry. The structural stability of the ceiling is remarkably high; the arch design is transferring the load directly into the basalt basement rock."

The team gathered in a tight circle, their six torch beams intersecting on the center of the chamber floor. There stood a structure that defied the natural randomness of the surrounding cave.

It was a circular basin, eight meters in diameter, constructed from cut blocks of black basalt fitted so precisely that even after three thousand years of seismic activity, the joints between the stones were less than a millimeter wide. The rim of the basin rose thirty centimeters above the clay floor, its upper surface polished to a mirror-like smoothness by ancient wear. In the center of the basin, half-submerged in ten centimeters of crystal-clear, standing water, lay a massive stone slab of pinkish-grey granite—a stone foreign to this immediate limestone region.

Geronimo stepped to the edge of the basin, his boots making a soft *crunch* on the gravel layer. He knelt, dipping his gloved fingers into the water. It was ice-cold, perhaps nine degrees Celsius, and remarkably soft to the touch, lacking the harsh mineral bite of the limestone runoff from above.

"Perennial spring," Geronimo said, observing the silent, unrippled surface of the water. "It’s fed from a deep phreatic aquifer, pushed up through the central granite plug of the mountain under artesian pressure. This isn't runoff. It’s deep water."

"Look at the orientation of the slab," André said, unholstering his compass and aligning it with the long axis of the granite block. "Two hundred and fourteen degrees magnetic. That’s not aligned with magnetic north, nor with geographic north. It points directly toward the winter solstice sunset as viewed through the mouth of the upper sinkhole four thousand years ago, accounting for the precession of the equinoxes."

Veerle reached into her field pack and retrieved a small, soft-bristled brush. She dropped to her knees beside the basin's outer edge, carefully clearing away a thin layer of dried mud that had accumulated against the lowest course of basalt blocks.

"This is where the folklore and the architectural reality merge," Veerle said, her voice filled with a quiet, intense focus. "The legends of the *Ammuttadori*—the night demon that sits upon the chest of those who sleep near the old ruins—were not merely tales to keep children away from deep holes. The *Ammuttadori* was the physical sensation of carbon dioxide pooling in low, unventilated basins like this during periods of atmospheric inversion. When the wind from the sea stalls, the heavy gas gathers in the depressions of the floor. If a person lay down here to sleep, they would suffer from hypoxia, experience intense paralysis and vivid hallucinations of a heavy weight pressing upon their lungs, and eventually die if not moved."

"A natural security system," Katia remarked, her eyes tracing the low-lying hollows around the basin floor. "Anyone who didn't know the atmospheric cycle of the cave would enter during an inversion, drop into the lower basin, and lose consciousness within ten minutes without ever realizing they were in danger. Simple, lethal, and entirely self-maintaining."

"And today?" Inga asked, checking her gas monitor again.

"The air current from the upper chimney is strong enough right now to keep the basin clear," Inga replied. "But if the temperature outside drops tonight and the air movement reverses, this basin could fill with gas within two hours. We have a clear window of six hours before the evening thermal shift."

Geronimo stood up, his gaze fixing on the far end of the chamber, where the basalt masonry did not merely line the walls, but formed a low, rectangular doorway cut directly into the vertical cliff face. The lintel stone was a single block of dark trachyte, four meters long and at least a meter thick, bearing a series of deep, angular petroglyphs that resembled stylized horns or inverted crescents.

"The doorway leads into the central fault," Geronimo said, pointing his light into the dark aperture beyond. "That is where the primary structural alignment should be. André, set up the laser transit here at the edge of the basin. We take our baseline measurement from the center of the granite slab to the midpoint of that lintel."

André set to work immediately, his hands moving with practiced, mechanical efficiency as he leveled the aluminum tripod, mounted the heavy red-anodized transit housing, and dialed in the digital level until the bullseye bubble was dead center. The thin red beam of the laser cut through the gloom of the chamber like a taut thread, striking the vertical center line of the distant door with a single, brilliant crimson dot.

"Distance: eighteen point four-two-six meters," André read off the digital display. "Elevation change: minus zero point three-four meters. The corridor beyond descends at an incline of precisely one in fifty—the classic gradient for ancient gravity-fed water conduits."

Maris approached the lintel, her hand resting against the rough, hammer-dressed face of the stone. She brought her face close to the join where the lintel met the upright jambs.

"This isn't just a door," Maris said, her voice tight with professional appraisal. "It’s a counterweight slot. Look inside the gap between the lintel and the side wall. There are vertical grooves lined with smooth, oil-impregnated schist plates. There was once a vertical stone portcullis here, designed to be raised or lowered by filling a copper bucket with water from the upper basin. A hydraulic lock."

"Is it jammed?" Katia asked, stepping up beside her, hand resting on her sidearm, her posture relaxed but ready for any structural shift.

"It’s not jammed; it’s dropped," Maris pointed to the base of the portal, where the heavy stone slab sat firmly wedged into a recessed channel cut into the bedrock floor. "The mechanism released thousands of years ago, probably when the original wooden or rope linkages rotted away. To pass through, we don't lift the stone—it weighs at least three metric tons. We go under the structural bypass that the early explorers dug through the softer shale layer on the right side."

She indicated a narrow, crawl-space opening near the floor, where the green chlorite shale had been broken away by hand tools, leaving an opening barely seventy centimeters wide and fifty centimeters high.

"Who made the bypass?" Veerle asked, examining the tool marks around the squeeze hole. "These aren't modern pick-axes. The marks are rounded, broad-bladed—like iron spades from the Punic or early Roman period."

"Treasury hunters from the Carthaginian era," Geronimo said, kneeling by the squeeze hole and shining his torch into the narrow passage. "They were following the same legends we are. But they didn't have the geological maps. They were digging blindly through the soft shale layers, looking for bronze offerings or gold ritual vessels."

"Did they find anything?" Inga asked.

"If they did, they didn't carry it all out," Geronimo replied, his light catching a metallic reflection six feet inside the narrow squeeze. "Look at that reflection. It’s not iron, and it’s not stone."

He lay flat on his chest, pulling himself into the narrow shale tunnel with steady, rhythmic hitches of his elbows and knees. The green shale was damp and crumbly, leaving dark, oily streaks on his tactical jacket. The team watched his boots vanish into the opening, the sound of his movements muffled by the soft, sound-dampening stone.

Thirty seconds passed in complete silence, broken only by the steady *drip-drip-drip* of the spring behind them and the faint, rhythmic ticking of the laser transit.

"I have the object," Geronimo's voice came back, strangely flattened by the narrow space. "It’s a heavy bronze casting, about thirty centimeters long. It was wedge-driven into the shale floor to act as a pivot point for a timber lever."

There was a sound of stone scraping against stone, followed by a low grunt of effort, and then Geronimo slid backward out of the squeeze, dragging a dark, heavy object by a loop of corroded metal.

He laid it on the clean flat surface of the basin rim. The team gathered round, their lights focused on the artifact.

It was a Nuragic bronze component—not a figurine, but a heavy, functional mechanical part. It consisted of a double-ringed socket connected to a solid, triangular wedge of bronze, green with a thick patina of copper carbonate, but completely uncorroded at its core. The surface was covered in fine, geometric hatchings, arranged in sets of five parallel lines each.

"A counterweight latch," Maris declared, her fingers carefully tracing the circular sockets. "This held the primary lever arm for the hydraulic system. When the water level in the cistern reached the fourth mark—the fourth pocked depression we saw on the outer wall—the weight of the filled basin would trip this latch, allowing the stone portcullis to drop and seal the inner gallery."

"Why seal it?" Katia asked, looking toward the dark opening of the squeeze. "To keep water in, or to keep something out?"

"Both," Veerle said, her voice dropping into the quiet register she used when synthesizing historical evidence. "In the Central Sardinian tradition, the water was not merely consumed; it was guarded. The subterranean sanctuaries were vaults for the community’s wealth, but also sacred points where the earth's internal power was believed to spill out. To close the gate was to lock the spring when the mountain's blood turned bad—when the mineral content became poisonous or the gas levels rose too high."

"And the inner gallery leads to the main vault," André said, looking at his transit line, which passed directly over the squeeze hole and vanished into the mountain's heart. "The survey coordinates from the 1894 document map a second, larger chamber three hundred meters further in, directly beneath the central tor of the Supramonte. That is where the structural axis aligns with the deep fracture zone."

Geronimo looked at his team. Their faces were clear, focused, calm. The initial physical fatigue of the descent had vanished, replaced by the sharp, methodical clarity that always came when the spatial geometry of a site began to reveal its underlying logic.

"Equipment check," Geronimo commanded softly. "Switch to personal oxygen monitors on all harnesses. We move through the bypass single-file. Inga takes the lead with the gas detector and hammer; Maris second with the structural shoring kit; André third with the laser line; then Veerle, Katia, and I bring up the rear. We maintain a five-meter distance between individuals once through the squeeze."

"Understood," Inga said, slipping her helmet back onto her head, clicking the chin strap tight, and unclipping her compact trenching tool. She dropped flat onto the clay, sliding her body into the narrow shale portal with smooth, deliberate motions.

One by one, the team followed. The process was slow, measured, and calculated. Each person dragged their personal gear pack behind them on a short tether, preventing their shoulders from wedging against the narrow, unstable ceiling of the shale layer.

The transition through the bypass took twelve minutes. On the other side, the shale gave way abruptly to a high, natural fissure in the crystalline trachyte—a vertical rift no more than ninety centimeters wide, but rising over fifteen meters into the dark height of the mountain above.

The air here was different. It was drier, colder—ten degrees Celsius—and held a distinct, sharp metallic smell, like copper coins held in a warm hand, mixed with the clean, ozone-like scent of static electricity.

"The air is clean," Inga called back, her voice echoing up the high, narrow rift like a note struck on a iron bar. "Oxygen twenty point nine percent. Zero toxic gases. The airflow here is coming up from below—from a deep vertical chimney."

Geronimo emerged from the squeeze last, dusting the green shale dust from his knees and elbows. He stood up, adjusted his headlamp, and looked up the narrow canyon of stone.

 around them, the walls of the rift were lined with millions of tiny quartz crystals that caught the beams of their headlamps and fractured the light into a thousand pinpricks of brilliant, white sparkle. But it was not the natural mineral wealth that drew their attention.

Directly ahead of them, embedded in the natural rift walls, was a continuous flight of steps cut into the living trachyte stone. Each step was precisely sixteen centimeters high and thirty-two centimeters deep—a ratio of one to two that was maintained with absolute, mathematical consistency as far up into the gloom as their lights could reach.

"Thirty-two steps visible in this first flight," André said, raising his laser pointer and aiming it up the staircase. The red dot hit a landing thirty meters above them. "The angle of ascent is twenty-six point five degrees. It’s an exact geometric progression."

Veerle reached out, her fingers skimming the smooth, vertical face of the fifth step.

"This is the *Scala de sas Aivas*—the Staircase of the Waters," she whispered, her voice echoing with soft reverent awe. "Mentioned in the medieval fragments of the *Condaghe de Santa Maria de Bonarcado*, but assumed by nineteenth-century historians to be a poetic myth invented by monks. It wasn't a myth. It was an engineering path leading from the deep aquifer to the upper sanctuary."

"And the floor?" Katia asked, pointing her light down at the narrow channel that ran alongside the right side of the steps.

A stone trough, ten centimeters wide and eight centimeters deep, was carved into the edge of the stair treads. It was dry, but the stone inside the trough was stained a deep, iridescent purple-black by manganese oxide deposits, indicating that water had flowed through it for centuries, if not millennia, at a constant, controlled depth.

"The water was carried up," Geronimo observed, studying the direction of the flow marks in the trough. "Not down. This wasn't a drainage channel; it was a capillary feeder channel, designed to draw water upward through siphon action or archimedean screws placed at intermediate landings."

He took three steps up the staircase, his boots making a clean, solid *clack* against the dense trachyte. The stone showed no sign of wear from modern footwear; only the shallow, smooth depressions in the center of each step recorded the passage of thousands of bare or leather-shod feet in antiquity.

"We advance in formation," Geronimo ordered, his voice taking on the sharp, spatial cadence that governed their movement in technical environments. "Keep to the left side of the treads to leave the water channel clear of debris. Maris, keep an eye on the structural integrity of the ceiling vault above the landing. André, record every ten steps for the volumetric model."

They began the climb. The movement was silent, rhythmic, and perfectly paced. Every step took precisely one second: *lift, plant, weight-shift, step*. The sound of six pairs of boots moving in unison created a steady, metronomic cadence that filled the narrow rift, echoing off the quartz-encrusted walls like the ticking of a grand clock inside the mountain.

At the twentieth step, the rift widened slightly, exposing a side niche carved into the left wall. Inside the niche stood a single, unadorned stone cylinder of white limestone—a stark, bright contrast against the dark trachyte matrix of the cave.

Veerle paused, her light illuminating the top surface of the cylinder.

"A sacred betyl," she said softly. "The * stone that sees*. It has no face, no arms, no inscriptions. Just the form itself, representing the eye of the mountain watching the passage of those who carry the water."

"Look at the base," Katia said, her tactical eye instantly spotting a spatial anomaly. "It’s not sitting on the floor of the niche. It’s mounted on a bronze pivot."

She reached out, her gloved fingers gently testing the side of the limestone cylinder. With a faint, dry *creak* of ancient metal that sounded like a sigh trapped in stone, the entire two-hundred-pound limestone pillar rotated three degrees on its vertical axis before coming to a firm stop against an hidden internal lug.

Deep within the stone wall behind the niche, a series of muted mechanical clacks reverberated—the sound of counterweights shifting within long-forgotten vertical shafts.

"Structural shift?" Inga asked instantly, her hand dropping to her gear harness as her eyes scanned the ceiling for falling dust or stress cracks.

"No," Maris said, monitoring her micro-strain gauge mounted on the wall jamb. "Zero pressure change in the main vault. It was a release mechanism for an upper air valve. Listen."

A low, gentle hiss filled the narrow staircase—the sound of fresh, cold air being drawn into the passage from an unseen vent above them. The scent of wild myrtle and dry, sun-warmed granite wafted down the steps, completely replacing the cold, wet iron smell of the lower basin.

"The upper chimney is open," Geronimo said, his light tracing the path of the staircase up to where it dissolved into a broad, vaulted opening fifty steps above. "The system is responding to our presence. The spatial locks are clearing as we ascend."

"Not clearing," Veerle corrected gently, her face illuminated by the reflected light of the quartz walls. "They are inviting. The structure was designed to be opened by those who knew the precise order of movement: the entry through the spring, the passage through the lower shale, the turning of the betyl. It’s an architectural ritual, expressed entirely through physical mechanics."

They continued their climb. Step thirty-two gave way to a broad, flat landing paved with large, hexagonal slabs of pink trachyte, arranged in a honeycomb pattern that extended twelve meters into a grand circular chamber beyond.

The team stepped onto the landing, their torch beams lifting simultaneously to reveal the full scope of the inner sanctuary.

It was a vast, dome-shaped hall, forty meters in diameter and at least twenty meters high at its central apex. The dome was not natural; it was a corbelled vault of immense proportions, constructed from thousands of roughly shaped trachyte blocks laid in overlapping concentric rings that narrowed as they rose toward the ceiling. At the exact apex of the dome, a circular opening two meters across let in a single, brilliant shaft of late-afternoon sunlight that cut through the gloom like a solid white pillar, striking the center of the chamber floor.

In the center of the sunbeam, resting on a raised, three-tiered platform of dark basalt, stood a massive bronze bowl, three meters in diameter, filled to the brim with clear, motionless water that mirrored the circular opening of the sky above with mathematical perfection.

"The Sun Vault of Tiscali," Veerle whispered, her hands dropping to her sides as she took in the scene. "The lost central chamber of the interior cult."

Geronimo stepped to the edge of the basalt platform, his shadow extending long and black across the hexagonal floor behind him. He looked up the shaft of light toward the tiny circle of blue sky visible through the top of the dome.

"The alignment is complete," Geronimo said, his voice quiet, measured, and absolute. "The sun will reach twenty-two degrees elevation in seven minutes. That is when the shaft hits the center of the lower conduit."

André was already setting up the second transit on the platform's lower tier, his pencil flying across the page of his notebook as he recorded the structural coordinates of the dome.

"The corbelled rings are stepped at a constant angle of fourteen degrees," André reported, his voice filled with geometric reverence. "The structural pressure is entirely balanced by the weight of the outer granite ring embedded in the mountain wall. It’s an inverted catenary curve—the strongest structural shape known to ancient architecture."

Katia moved to the perimeter of the room, her eyes systematically checking the five small archways that branched off from the main chamber like the spokes of a wheel. Each archway led into a dark, narrow corridor that extended deeper into the mountain's core.

"All side corridors are dry and structurally sound," Katia reported, her voice calm and steady. "No signs of modern intrusion. The dust layers on the floors are undisturbed, at least three centimeters thick, composed of fine wind-blown silt and decayed organic material from the upper vent."

Inga set her pack down at the foot of the basalt platform, opening the main compartment to retrieve the deep-survey sounder and the multi-spectral camera assembly.

"We have three hours of daylight left through the central shaft," Inga said, checking the solar angle with her watch. "After fifteen hundred hours, the sun drops below the western rim of the Supramonte, and the light shaft will vanish until tomorrow morning."

"Then we begin the volumetric mapping now," Geronimo said, stepping up to the edge of the great bronze bowl. 

He looked down into the water. Deep beneath the clear surface, resting on the smooth bronze floor of the basin, lay a complex pattern of inlaid silver wire, forming a map of the surrounding mountain ranges, the rivers, and the subterranean water channels that connected them all like the veins of a living body.

"Look at the silver lines," Geronimo said softly, gesturing for Veerle and André to join him at the basin's rim. "It’s not a map of the surface. It’s a three-dimensional topological map of the subterranean hydrography of Central Sardinia. Every spring, every sinkhole, every deep karst system between the Tirso and the Flumineddu is laid out here in scale."

Veerle leaned over the water, her eyes following the intricate, shimmering lines of silver that caught the vertical sunbeam and reflected small, dancing flecks of light onto the corbelled ceiling above.

"The legend was true," Veerle said quietly, her voice echoing softly in the great, sunlit vault. "They didn't just map the mountains. They mapped the water that made the mountains alive. And we are standing at the exact center of the map."

Geronimo adjusted his wrist chronometer, noting the precise moment the center of the solar disk crossed the edge of the upper sky-vent. The light in the room shifted, turning from brilliant white to a deep, warm gold that illuminated every grain of the trachyte walls, every crystal of quartz, and every line of silver lying silently beneath the surface of the ancient water.
