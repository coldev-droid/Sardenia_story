# BOOK I, CHAPTER 1: THE BOOK THAT FORGOT FIRE

The frost in El Raval did not arrive as a drop in physical temperature. It arrived as an erasure of memory, a coldness that stole the history of warmth from the mind before it ever touched the skin.

It was a suffocating July night in Barcelona. The heavy, wet Mediterranean heat pressed against the labyrinthine streets of the old port like a damp wool blanket, weaving itself into the very pores of the gothic stone. The air smelled of stale beer, sea salt, the metallic tang of the freight yards, and the rotting sweetness of discarded fruit from the distant stalls of La Boqueria. The city was alive with the frantic, desperate energy of a summer night, tourists and locals alike seeking refuge in the narrow shadows of the Gothic Quarter, their voices echoing off centuries-old walls.

Yet, inside the ruined back office of Elara’s antiquarian bookshop, Geronimo’s breath plumed in the air. 

He sat on the edge of an overturned mahogany desk, his broad shoulders hunched under the weight of an invisible, crushing atmosphere. The shop, once a sanctuary of leather-bound history and quiet academic pursuit, had been ransacked three days ago. Books were shredded, their leather bindings torn and their vellum pages scattered like dead leaves across the floor. Floorboards had been pried upward like broken teeth, revealing the dark, dust-choked spaces beneath the 19th-century architecture. 

Elara was gone. 

She had left behind only blood—a dark, dried smear across the oak baseboard that Geronimo could not stop staring at—and a series of cryptic, damaged ledgers. She was a mentor, a historian, and a guardian of the things that the modern world had agreed to forget. Now, she was a casualty.

The rest of the team—Maris, Veerle, Inga, and André—were scattered across Europe, chasing phantom logistical leads and dead drops that the enemy had deliberately seeded to separate them. Maris was stuck in a safehouse in Prague, trying to decode a set of falsified transit manifests that led nowhere. Veerle and Inga had gone dark somewhere near the Swiss border, hunting a courier who didn't exist. André was in London, dealing with the bureaucratic nightmare of their burned identities, fighting a quiet war against algorithms and frozen bank accounts. The network had been systematically dismantled, a surgical strike designed to isolate the vanguard. 

Geronimo and Katia were completely alone, holding the only genuine piece of the puzzle in a city that felt increasingly like a cage. 

Geronimo stared at the cheap glass of water resting on the surviving corner of the desk. A minute ago, the water had been tepid, gathering condensation in the stifling Catalan heat. Now, it was frozen solid. The ice had formed instantly, expanding and cracking the cheap glass with a sharp, violent *tink* that sounded like a gunshot in the silent room. But the air in the room wasn't cold. His skin was sweating underneath his canvas jacket. The ambient temperature was easily thirty degrees Celsius. The frost was localized, an impossible phenomenon radiating from the heavy, lead-lined lockbox resting on the center of the desk.

He stood up, his heavy boots crunching over the shredded parchment and broken glass. The shop was a graveyard of human history. Elara had dedicated her life to the preservation of lost mythologies, hoarding texts that the academic world had deemed heretical or simply insane. She had understood that myth was not fiction; it was dormant history, waiting for the right catalyst to awaken. Now, her life's work was reduced to confetti. The violence of the search indicated desperation. Whoever took her wasn't just looking for information; they were looking for an object. 

And they hadn't found it.

Katia stepped into the dim room, a shadow detaching itself from the hallway. She moved with absolute silence, her boots avoiding the debris with practiced, almost unconscious grace. She was dressed in dark tactical canvas, the sleeves rolled up to reveal the dense, soot-inked protective tattoos winding up her forearms. The intricate patterns of the ink seemed to writhe in the low light, a testament to her deep integration into the occult underbelly of Europe. Her face was pale, tight with a pragmatic dread she rarely allowed to surface.

"The perimeter is clear," Katia said, her voice a low, gravelly whisper that barely disturbed the dust motes dancing in the beam of the streetlamp outside. "No watchers on Carrer de l'Hospital. The local police are still treating the break-in as a standard burglary. They won't look closer. The wards on the front door are holding, but they are degrading fast. Whatever is in that box is eating the ambient kinetic energy of the room."

Geronimo nodded slowly, his eyes never leaving the lockbox. "It's not just energy. It's memory. It's a localized amnesiac field targeting thermodynamics."

He reached out and struck a wooden match from a box in his pocket. The sulfur flared, a tiny, brilliant orange beacon in the gloom. He held it close to the lockbox, watching the flame dance. Slowly, he moved the match closer to the lead casing. 

The flame didn't blow out. It froze. 

The chemical combustion halted mid-burn, the orange fire petrifying into a rigid, fragile sculpture of light, before turning a dull, lifeless gray and crumbling to ash against the tabletop. Geronimo felt a sudden, terrifying void in his mind—he could no longer remember what the heat of a fire felt like. The sensation of warmth, the fundamental biological memory of heat, was simply gone, replaced by a terrifying, hollow numbness that made him want to scream. 

"It's deleting the history of warmth," Geronimo whispered, dropping the charred stick. He rubbed his temples, trying to force the memory of a campfire, of a hot stove, back into his brain. It was like reaching into a dark closet and finding the shelves completely bare. 

Katia's eyes widened fractionally. She stepped closer, her posture tense, ready to draw the weapon at her hip. "Open it. We need to confirm what Elara died to protect. If it's what we think it is, we need to know now."

Geronimo reached into his tactical vest and pulled out a heavy, cured leather cloth, lined with a thin mesh of silver wire. He wrapped it carefully around his hand, ensuring no skin would make direct contact with the contents. With his other hand, he keyed the combination into the lockbox. The heavy steel tumblers clicked into place, and the lid sprang open.

Inside, resting on a bed of crushed rock salt and silver shavings, was a piece of black glass. 

It wasn't the Obsidian Eye. They hadn't earned that yet. It was merely a splinter. 

The shard was roughly the size of a thumb, jagged and asymmetrical, its surface so intensely dark that it seemed to absorb the dim light of the room, creating a miniature event horizon on the desktop. It was not a polished gemstone; it was a violent fracture, torn from a larger whole. 

"It's a lens," Geronimo murmured, the realization dawning on him with the weight of a physical blow. Even through the thick hide of the glove, the erasure bit into his hand, making his metacarpal bones feel as though they had been buried in permafrost for a century. "The true Eye is still on the island. Somewhere under Monte d'Accoddi. This fragment... it’s trying to sync with it. It’s pulling. It's trying to establish a line of sight."

Katia stared at the splinter, her breath catching in her throat as she cross-referenced the artifact with the scattered ruins of Elara's life. "Elara's ledgers. The ones that weren't destroyed by the acid. I spent the last three hours decoding the marginalia while you slept. She wasn't translating a text; she was tracking a transit route."

Katia moved to the battered remains of Elara’s workbench, sweeping aside a pile of shredded 18th-century maps to reveal a massive, heavily annotated nautical chart of the Western Mediterranean. The chart was ancient, its edges fraying, but the ink tracking the maritime routes was fresh, a stark crimson against the yellowed parchment. 

"Look at the trajectory," Katia said, her finger tracing a jagged line from the Spanish coast across the Balearic Sea toward the northwestern coast of Sardinia. "The enemy isn't just looking for the Eye. They are already attempting to excavate Monte d'Accoddi. But the ziggurat is sealed by a geographic lock. A terrestrial ward that cannot be broken by physical force. They need a resonance key to bypass it. This splinter is that key."

Geronimo leaned over the table, the freezing sensation receding slightly as he stepped away from the lockbox. "If they get this splinter to the island, they can open the primary vault. The Eye will wake completely."

"Exactly," Katia confirmed, her eyes scanning the complex web of dates and tidal charts Elara had scrawled in the margins. "Elara intercepted this shard. She brought it here to break the resonance, to try and blind the true Eye by hiding the lens in a city built on different mythic foundations. But she ran out of time. The Grimaldi lines ferry—the *Cruise Roma*—leaves Port Vell tonight, bound for Porto Torres. The enemy knows the shard is in Barcelona. They are locking down the airports, monitoring the train stations. The maritime route is the only blind spot left, and it's closing rapidly."

Geronimo looked back at the splinter. The air around the lockbox was shimmering slightly, a visual distortion caused by the total absence of thermal memory. "If we take that shard onto a civilian ferry, we are carrying a beacon directly toward the most dangerous mythological hot zone in Europe. The true Eye is under the ziggurat, broadcasting a massive, vital energy signature. It's a dinner bell. And the things that answer that bell are not human."

"We have no choice," Katia said, her tone absolute, hardened by years of surviving the impossible. "If we stay in El Raval, they will find us, and they will take the key. If we abandon the splinter, we fail Elara, and we fail the mission. The vanguard is isolated. We have to secure the primary artifact before the enemy breaches Monte d'Accoddi."

Geronimo nodded, the grim reality settling into his bones. They were walking into a trap, driven by necessity and the desperate hope that they could outmaneuver an ancient, waking intelligence. 

"What about defense?" Geronimo asked, gesturing toward the lockbox. "If customs checks our bags and finds a sliver of rock that gives them localized amnesia, we’re not getting across the Balearic."

Katia reached into her heavy canvas duffel bag and pulled out a long, curved object wrapped in oiled cloth. She unwrapped it carefully, revealing a rusted, heavily pitted iron sickle. The inner curve of the ancient blade was completely dulled, flat, and totally toothless. It was an agricultural implement, ancient and brutal, radiating a quiet, stubborn history that felt entirely out of place in a modern European city. 

"I made a detour in Marseille before meeting you here," Katia explained, her voice dropping to a clinical register. "I met with a black-market archivist specializing in Campidanese witchcraft trials. This is a *faciola*. It’s from Villacidro. *Sa Bidda De Is Cogas*. The witches' village."

Geronimo eyed the rusted tool. It clashed violently with her modern tactical gear, a stark reminder of the deep, ancient roots of their war. "Villacidro is deep south in Sardinia. We're docking in Porto Torres, in the extreme north."

"Folklore isn't bound by municipal borders, Geronimo," Katia said, running her thumb over the blunt edge of the sickle. "Magic migrates when it's hungry. The true Eye is acting as a massive gravitational well. The Cogas—the coastal entities, the identity thieves—will be drawn to the transit route. They ride the psychic wake of human migration. They don't fly on brooms; they don't turn into bats. They steal memories. They replace a person's identity until the host is entirely overwritten."

She held the sickle up, the dim light catching the pitted iron. "It’s a mathematical lock. The Cogas are bound by rigid mythological laws. If you present them with a sickle, they are compelled to count the teeth on the blade. Their entire existence is predicated on consumption and numeration. But their minds operate strictly on base-seven logic. They count to seven, lose their place, and are forced to start over. It traps them in an infinite cognitive loop. Because this blade is toothless, it creates a paradox. It should paralyze them entirely."

"A cognitive trap," Geronimo mused, the tactical logic appealing to his military background. "If they confront us on the water, we lock them in a loop and dump them overboard."

"That's the theory," Katia said, wrapping the sickle back in the oiled cloth and securing it to her tactical harness. "But theories have a habit of failing when exposed to the raw gravity of the Obsidian Eye. We need to move."
